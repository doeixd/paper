## 1. Regime Individuation and Hidden Circularity

**The problem in full.** The paper's anti-circularity strategy is procedural: fix regime, horizon, and admissibility *before* scoring partitions. But regime individuation is not itself a theory-neutral act. Identifying "weekday commuter traffic in one metropolitan corridor" as a regime already presupposes that traffic-level variables are the right grain at which to carve dynamical phases. Two scientists could disagree about where one regime ends and another begins — not because one is gerrymandering, but because regime boundaries are themselves macro-level descriptions that face a version of the same closure question. This threatens a regress: you need closure to identify macro-objects, but you need regime identification to test closure, and regime identification looks like it already involves macro-ontological commitments.

**How to address it.** The author should do three things.

First, distinguish between *regime specification* and *partition specification* explicitly and argue that they involve different kinds of commitment with different vulnerability profiles. A regime is a characterization of background dynamical conditions — boundary conditions, energy scales, coupling strengths, parameter ranges. These are typically identifiable through independently measurable physical quantities (temperature ranges, Reynolds numbers, flow rates, institutional enforcement intensities) that don't require prior commitment to the specific macro-partition being tested. The author could argue that regime individuation draws on lower-level or cross-cutting physical indicators rather than on the candidate partition itself. This doesn't eliminate judgment, but it does break the tight circle the objection alleges.

For instance, in the traffic example, "weekday commuter traffic" isn't identified by presupposing that lane-segment density is the right macro-variable. It's identified by clock time, day of week, total vehicle counts, and road network topology — quantities that are available independently of whether you partition by lane-flow or vehicle-ID clusters. The author should make this kind of independence argument explicit and general: regime specifications should be *cross-partition accessible*, meaning that competing candidate partitions can agree on the regime characterization even while disagreeing about which coarse-graining closes within it.

Second, the author should acknowledge that the independence is imperfect in some domains and treat that imperfection as informative rather than fatal. There will be cases — probably in biology and social science especially — where regime individuation and macro-partition choice are entangled. When that happens, the framework should recognize it as a source of *evidential weakness* rather than trying to pretend the entanglement isn't there. The practical consequence is that entangled cases should receive at most qualified verdicts. You could formalize this with something like: if the closure verdict is sensitive to defensible alternative regime specifications, and those alternatives are motivated independently of partition preference, then the verdict ceiling is "qualified" regardless of how clean the closure looks within any single regime specification. This turns a potential objection into a built-in diagnostic.

Third, the author should address the regress worry head-on. The regress — needing closure to identify objects, needing regimes to test closure, needing objects to identify regimes — is genuine only if every step requires the same kind of justification. But regime identification is an empirically constrained act of domain characterization, not an objecthood verdict. Scientists identify dynamical regimes through phase diagrams, bifurcation analysis, dimensional analysis, and similar tools that operate on measurable quantities without first settling which macro-objects exist. The author could frame this as a *bootstrapping* process rather than a vicious circle: initial regime characterizations are provisional, closure testing refines them, and the process stabilizes when regime boundaries and partition verdicts reach reflective equilibrium. This is structurally similar to how Goodman treats the relationship between rules of inference and particular inferences — neither is foundational, but the process of mutual adjustment is not viciously circular. The author should cite Goodman explicitly here and argue that the bootstrapping is disciplined by the same procedural constraints (fix before scoring, restart on revision, report sensitivity) that govern the rest of the framework.

## 2. Multiple Realizability and the Relationship to Classical Autonomy Arguments

**The problem in full.** Putnam's and Fodor's arguments for the autonomy of special sciences already established that higher-level kinds can be explanatorily indispensable precisely because they abstract over heterogeneous micro-realizations. The closure criterion looks like it's doing very similar work: a macro-partition that screens off within-class micro-differences is, in effect, a multiply realizable kind whose realizers don't matter for the target dynamics. So what does closure add? If the answer is "nothing much," the paper's novelty claim is weaker than presented. If the answer is "something important," it needs to be spelled out.

**How to address it.** The author should argue that closure is *strictly more demanding* than multiple realizability and that this additional demand is what does the anti-gerrymandering work.

Multiple realizability, as classically formulated, is a property of a kind-predicate: the kind is multiply realizable if it can be instantiated by different micro-configurations. But this is a purely extensional condition. It tells you that different microstates fall under the same macro-label. It does not tell you whether those microstates *behave the same way* at the macro level going forward. A gerrymandered kind — say, "objects that are either electrons in spin-up or hurricanes in Category 3" — is multiply realizable in the trivial sense that it has heterogeneous realizers. But it utterly fails closure because the realizers have completely different transition profiles. The disjunctive kind has no autonomous dynamics.

The author should make this distinction sharp. Multiple realizability is a *necessary* feature of closed macro-kinds (since closure entails that different microstates within a macroclass produce the same macro-transitions, which means the macro-kind is multiply realized). But it is not *sufficient*, because multiple realizability says nothing about dynamical coherence. Closure adds the dynamical requirement: the realizers must not only fall under the same label but must *go to the same macro-places* under the declared constraints. This is the gap that Fodor's original argument leaves open, and it's precisely the gap through which gerrymandered kinds slip.

The author could strengthen this by noting that the classical autonomy literature has always been somewhat embarrassed by the question of *which* multiply realizable kinds deserve realist treatment. Not every disjunctive predicate that happens to be multiply realized is a natural kind. The standard response — appeal to causal or nomic role — is gesturing at something like closure without formalizing it. The present paper can be framed as making explicit what the autonomy tradition always needed but never fully articulated: the dynamical transition condition that separates genuine higher-level kinds from accidental disjunctions that happen to share a label.

There's also a productive connection to Shapiro's (2000) critique of multiple realizability arguments, which argues that many purported cases of multiple realization are either trivial (different physical tokens of the same relevant type) or involve kinds that are not genuinely doing the same functional work. The closure criterion provides a principled way to adjudicate exactly this dispute: check whether the alleged realizers produce the same macro-transitions under admissible interventions. If they do, the multiple realization is genuine and dynamically significant. If they don't, Shapiro's skepticism is vindicated for that case. The author should position the framework as resolving, or at least disciplining, this long-standing impasse.

Conversely, if the macro-partition gives you a factor of ten and micro-features add two percent, the leakage is negligible for most purposes. This ratio-based framing doesn't eliminate judgment, but it makes the judgment *about the same quantity* (predictive or interventional gain) rather than about an abstract threshold, and it makes comparisons across domains more tractable.

The right analogy is something like biological fitness: everyone agrees that fitness is real and explanatorily important, but no one thinks there's a sharp boundary between "fit" and "unfit." The graded verdict structure mirrors the graded metaphysical property. What matters is not that the boundaries are sharp but that the *ordering* is stable: if partition A consistently shows lower leakiness and greater perturbation stability than partition B across diagnostics, the verdict ordering (A gets stronger standing than B) should be robust even if exactly where "qualified" ends and "robust" begins is somewhat conventional. - An *indeterminate* verdict case: a contested social-scientific kind — perhaps "middle class" as an economic category — where different operationalizations produce divergent closure results, regime specification is entangled with partition choice, and verdicts are highly sensitive to admissibility class.

These illustrations would show that the verdict structure is doing real discriminative work, even if the exact boundaries carry some conventional element. They would also preempt the worry that the framework only produces clean results in cases (like thermodynamics) where we already knew the answer.
