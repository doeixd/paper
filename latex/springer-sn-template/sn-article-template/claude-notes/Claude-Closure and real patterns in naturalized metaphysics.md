This is a sharp and well-constructed piece of naturalized metaphysics. Here's what I think works, what's genuinely novel, and where I'd push back.

**What it does well**

The core move is elegant: take Dennett's real patterns framework, which has always been vulnerable to the "too permissive" and "secretly instrumentalist" objections, and plug the gap with a single formal criterion — closure of macro-transition structure (lumpability). The hurricane vs. shoe-Neptune contrast in the introduction is a clean way to make the problem vivid, and the paper earns the right to its formal machinery by motivating it intuitively first.

The graded approach through approximate lumpability and leakiness is the right call. A binary "closed or not" criterion would be almost as problematic as having no criterion at all, since virtually no real-world macro-object has perfect closure. The leakiness spectrum lets you say something principled about flames, ecosystems, and other borderline cases without either promoting them to full objecthood or dismissing them entirely.

I also think the distinction between *entailed* and *stable* patterns (Section 5.4) is doing quiet but important philosophical work. It cleanly dissolves the Laplacean worry — yes, an omniscient being could track the shoe-Neptune composite, but trackability isn't the same as transition autonomy.

**What's genuinely novel**

The paper's real contribution isn't any single piece (Dennett, lumpability, computational mechanics, interventionism are all well-established) but the *joint deployment* as an exclusion criterion with explicit failure conditions. Most papers in this space propose criteria for when something *is* a real pattern; this one is equally interested in principled exclusion, which is harder and more useful. The phlogiston example in 6.2 — reframing theory failure as the signature of a non-autonomous partition requiring constant micro-bookkeeping — is a nice application.

The answer to Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (Section 8.4) is provocative and compressed in the right way. Life as a *sufficient but not necessary* condition for composition, grounded in closure rather than biological essentialism, is a genuinely interesting position.

**Where I'd push**

A few concerns:

The **regime-relativity** is both the framework's greatest strength and its most philosophically exposed flank. The paper acknowledges this but perhaps underestimates the pressure. If objecthood is always relative to a regime, horizon *L*, and intervention class *ℐ*, then one might worry that the framework hasn't escaped instrumentalism so much as relocated it. The paper says analysts choose targets but can't choose whether closure holds — but choosing the regime-horizon-intervention triple is doing a lot of the work that "choosing whether to call it real" was doing before. The admissibility constraints (epistemic, dynamical, explanatory) are supposed to block this, but they themselves seem to require judgment calls that could reintroduce the flexibility the criterion was meant to eliminate.

The **interventional closure** requirement is the ontologically ambitious part, but the paper concedes (Section 5.5) that in many domains direct interventions are sparse or ethically constrained, so predictive closure serves as the operational proxy. This is honest, but it means that in practice the criterion often falls back to something close to the compression-prediction view it's trying to surpass. The gap between the criterion's aspirations and its typical implementation could be made more explicit.

The **non-Markov extension** (Section 9.3) is gestured at but not worked out. Replacing *X_t* with an enriched history state *H_t* and rerunning the same tests sounds clean in principle, but choosing the right history embedding is a substantial modeling decision that could smuggle in the very kinds of analyst discretion the paper wants to constrain. This deserves more than a paragraph.

Finally, the paper is almost entirely **defensive** in its engagement with social kinds and institutions. The legal adjudication example at the end is suggestive but brief. Given that social ontology is where permissiveness worries bite hardest — are corporations real macro-objects? property rights? money? — this feels like the paper's most important frontier, and it's left almost entirely to future work.

**Overall**

This reads like a serious, well-positioned intervention in the Dennett/emergence/coarse-graining literature. It's technically informed without being gratuitously formal, and it maintains a clear argumentative thread. The writing is disciplined — it resists the temptation to overclaim. If I were reviewing it, I'd recommend development of the non-Markov case and the social-kinds extension, and I'd want the author to confront the regime-relativity worry more directly. But the central argument — that closure of transition structure is the missing criterion that makes real-patterns realism defensible — strikes me as sound and worth developing.

**Social kinds** are categories that exist because of human social activity rather than physical structure alone. Things like money, corporations, marriages, laws, borders, universities, property rights. The twenty-dollar bill in your wallet is physically just paper and ink — what makes it *money* is a vast web of institutions, enforcement, shared expectations, and legal frameworks.

The reason they matter for this paper is that they're the hardest test case for any ontological criterion. Is a corporation a real macro-object? It has something like transition autonomy — knowing Apple's financial state, contractual obligations, and governance structure lets you predict what Apple will do next without tracking every employee's neurons. But its existence depends on courts, records, and enforcement in a way that a hurricane doesn't. If the legal system collapses, the corporation stops functioning as a causal entity even though the people and buildings are still there. The paper gestures at this but doesn't work it out, which is why I flagged it as unfinished business.

**Non-Markov processes** are systems where the future depends on more than just the current state — history matters.

A Markov process has no memory. If you know where the system is *right now*, that's all you need to predict what happens next. How it got there is irrelevant. A simple example: a fair die roll. Each roll is independent of previous rolls.

Most real systems aren't like this. Consider an immune system responding to a pathogen. Its behavior depends not just on its current biochemical state but on its *history* of past infections, which shaped its repertoire of antibodies. Or consider a person deciding whether to trust someone — their decision depends on accumulated experience, not just the present moment.

The paper's formal criterion is built on the Markov case because that's where lumpability is cleanly defined. You partition microstates into macro-classes, check whether members of each class have identical transition probabilities to other classes, done. But if the micro-process has memory — if history matters — then you can't just check the current state. You need to ask whether macro-*history* screens off micro-*history* for macro-futures, which is a harder problem.

The paper says you handle this by enriching the state to include relevant history, then rerunning the same tests. That's technically correct — it's what computational mechanics does — but it pushes a lot of difficulty into the question of *how much history to include and how to summarize it*, which is a substantial modeling choice. That's why I said the non-Markov extension is more of a promissory note than a worked-out result.

In short: social kinds test whether the framework can handle entities whose existence depends on institutional scaffolding, and non-Markov cases test whether it can handle systems with memory. Both are where the real world lives, and both are where the paper is thinnest.
