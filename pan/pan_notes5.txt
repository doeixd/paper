The biggest structural issue is repetition. The paper repeatedly says versions of:

first-person evidence warrants only what it discloses;
hidden phenomenal ontology must be defended theoretically;
panpsychism overdraws on experience;
the datum is real but finite;
the gap reappears between hidden base and actual consciousness.

These are good claims, but they appear so often that the paper sometimes feels like it is circling rather than advancing. You could cut perhaps 20–30% without losing substance. The argument would become more forceful.

I would consider restructuring around five moves:

The datum: first-person experience establishes consciousness, not its metaphysical status.
The constraint: first-person warrant does not transfer to hidden phenomenal posits.
The abstraction problem: “bare subjectivity” and “proto-experience” become less warranted the thinner they get.
The Russellian vacancy problem: structure/dynamics may leave a role open, but first-person acquaintance does not show that phenomenality fills it.
The burden shift: panpsychism may still be true, but its hidden ontology must explain enough to justify itself.

Right now, some sections overlap heavily. “Scope and Bracketing,” “The Introspective Warrant Constraint,” “The Failure of Warrant Transfer,” and parts of “Explanatory Burden” all make closely related points. You could either condense them or make each section do more distinct work.

The most philosophically delicate section is the treatment of pure awareness. I think the dilemma is good:

If pure awareness has some phenomenal character, then it is not bare characterless subjectivity.
If it has no phenomenal character, then it is not disclosed as experience.

That is strong. But be careful with the reportability argument. There are really two issues:

Private acquaintance: what the subject allegedly knows in the episode.
Public evidence: what philosophers can use as evidence through reports.

You notice this distinction, which is good. But the section risks sounding as though reportability limits private evidence itself. A defender of pure awareness will push back: “The report is fallible, but the episode itself was directly known.” Your best reply is not mainly about reportability; it is the dilemma above. Even granting the private episode, either it has some phenomenal character or it does not. The reportability point should be secondary.

The Russellian monism section is also strong, but it could use a sharper distinction between two claims:

physics leaves intrinsic/categorical nature unspecified;
consciousness is our only acquaintance with intrinsic/categorical nature.

The second claim is doing a lot of work. The panpsychist may say: “We know phenomenal character not merely as an object but as intrinsic nature itself.” Your reply should press: do we know it as intrinsic nature, or do we know it as phenomenal character and then interpret it as intrinsic nature? That is a key hinge. I would foreground it.

The most important likely objection is this:

The first-person datum does not need to disclose micro-experience directly. It only needs to disclose the nature of phenomenal properties, which then gives us reason to identify those properties with the categorical grounds of physical structure.

Your answer should be:

Acquaintance with phenomenal character may reveal what pain or color-experience is like, but it does not reveal that the property so encountered plays the categorical-grounding role. That role assignment is theoretical.

That is one of the paper’s best points. Make it central.

A second likely objection:

Panpsychism does not claim to explain the structure of human consciousness solely from micro-experience. Organization explains structure; micro-experience explains why there is anything it is like at all.

You already address this. But the reply could be tightened:

If organization explains unity, temporality, contents, attention, reportability, and valence, then the panpsychist must specify the residual explanatory contribution of fundamental phenomenality. If the contribution is merely “preventing emergence from the wholly non-phenomenal,” then the view has supplied a metaphysical comfort, not yet an explanation of the consciousness we undergo.

That is a good pressure point.

Stylistically, the prose is clear, controlled, and appropriately serious. But it sometimes sounds too much like a dissertation chapter: lots of literature positioning, many careful qualifications, repeated deferrals. For journal-article force, I would make the central argumentative line more visible and move some literature adjacency into footnotes or shorter paragraphs.
