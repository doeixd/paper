Why Panpsychism Doesn't Make Sense to Me
If first-person experience is the foundation for evidence, then panpsychism should not get to posit things that first-person experience never discloses
Patrick
May 16, 2026





Why Panpsychism Doesn’t Make Sense to Me
Consciousness is what it feels like to be something. Mere subjectivity. The bare feeling of being. Panpsychism says everything, at bottom, is that.

The pitch is appealing. Consciousness is the one thing we know from the inside. We know it firsthand in a way we don’t know mass or charge — we don’t know what physical reality is like in itself, but we know our own minds. So putting consciousness at the bottom of everything isn’t positing some occult property. It’s recognizing that what we know best is what’s actually fundamental.

The more I sit with it, the more it falls apart. Because the view keeps having to override the very thing that gave it its appeal: first-person experience.

If we’re talking about consciousness, first-person experience is the only data we have. Everything we know about consciousness in others is inferred by analogy from our own case. A theory of consciousness that keeps telling me I’m wrong about my own experience has cut itself loose from its only anchor.

Ya need something to feel
When you say it feels like something to be me, what exactly are you feeling besides mere subjectivity? It’s circular. Atomic, on its own, it doesn’t quite make sense. How can you feel what it’s like to be you? What exactly are you feeling? It’s substanceless. Empty. It bottoms out.

If you remove everything there is to feel, nothing is left to feel. Panpsychists say the only content of the experience is the experience itself. What does that even mean? Feel what? You feel what exactly? What is mere subjectivity with no content besides being a witness to subjectivity itself? Do you see what I’m getting at here?

Here’s where the argument from firsthand knowledge turns on itself. What we actually know firsthand is not bare subjectivity. It’s structured experience — feelings of things, qualities arranged in fields, awareness with content. When you actually attend to your consciousness, you never find pure mere-feeling. You find a feeling of warmth, a sense of time passing, a thought taking shape, an identity.

So the panpsychist has a fork. Either put structured experience at the bottom — in which case it can’t be fundamental, because the structure needs explaining — or put “bare subjectivity” at the bottom, which is an abstraction we’ve never actually encountered in any first-person experience. The warrant collapses either way. What you call fundamental either has too much in it, or so little that you don’t actually know it firsthand.

Picture a fan where each one of the blades meets at a mathematical point. Each blade represents one of your senses: vision, hearing, your sense of time, your memory, your sense of touch, your sense of self. The breeze the fan produces is like consciousness. Take one blade away and you still get a breeze. Take another, still a breeze. Throw out sight. Throw out memory. Smaller breeze, but still a breeze. Once you’ve taken away every blade though — every sense, every input — no more breeze. Subjectivity isn’t a feeling on its own. It’s the shape feelings happen inside of. Empty it out. You get nothing.

The more careful panpsychists, will say they aren’t claiming atoms have a recognizable subjectivity. They’re claiming the hidden inner nature of physical reality is “proto-experiential” — something experience-like, but not full experience. Fine. But that’s entirely speculative. We accept “mass” and “charge” as fundamental because they figure in predictive equations, unify phenomena, do theoretical work. Fundamental experientiality does none of that. It’s introduced solely to answer “where does consciousness come from?” and then doesn’t actually answer it. That’s a placeholder.

And this is where the burden matters. I’m not saying panpsychism is impossible. Lots of things are possible. Maybe every particle has some hidden inner glow. Maybe dreamless sleep is full of tiny unfelt feelings. Maybe there are experience-like properties everywhere, too thin to notice, too private to test, too basic to explain. But possible isn’t enough. Especially here. Consciousness is the thing whose evidence is supposed to be first-person experience. So if a theory asks me to posit consciousness where experience gives me nothing, where science gives me no independent test, and where the posit explains no actual feature of my mind, then I don’t know what the argument is anymore. It’s not false because I can disprove it. It fails because nothing strong enough has been offered to make me believe it.

Sleep
Most panpsychist views imply you stay conscious during dreamless sleep — you just don’t remember it. However, to me, if I’m conscious of anything, it’s that: When I’m asleep, it doesn’t feel like anything to be me (our definition of consciousness).

Either the panpsychist is using “conscious” in a sense cut loose from the original meaning — what-it’s-like-to-be-something — in which case they’re no longer talking about the thing we wanted explained. Or they’re making an unfalsifiable claim about experience that left no trace in memory, behavior, or report.

You might try to test this externally. Show that sleeping people respond to stimuli, that there’s neural processing going on. But responsiveness isn’t consciousness. A philosophical zombie would respond to stimuli too. Behavior underdetermines consciousness. Even if a sleeping person reacts, that tells us nothing about whether there was anything it was like to be them. The panpsychist has no first-person ground for the sleep claim and no scientific ground either. It’s pure stipulation/speculation again.

When the fan stops, the breeze stops. There’s no quieter breeze waiting somewhere to be detected. That’s what it’s like to be asleep imo.

Time
If consciousness is fundamental, what is its time-scale? How small a slice of time does it happen in? Am I conscious at every unimaginably tiny instant?

Again, if I’m conscious of anything: Clearly not. My visual field takes something like 100 milliseconds of processing before it shows up in experience. Things faster than that don’t enter consciousness at all. You can flash an image too quickly for me to register, and there’s nothing it feels like for me to see it. I didn’t see it. It didnt show up in my consciousness fast enough to feel like anything.

That’s what you’d expect if consciousness arises from the work the brain is doing. A panpsychist can reply that human consciousness has a biological time-scale because the brain integrates more basic experience-like properties at its own speed. But then: every actual feature of my consciousness — content, structure, whether an experience feels good or bad, temporal flow, sense of self — is already accounted for by the processing. There’s no residual feature for the fundamental experiential layer to explain. It’s decorative.

And again, the panpsychist’s reply is overriding my first-person testimony. I’m telling you there’s nothing it’s like to perceive the sub-100ms flash. The theory says I’m conscious of it anyway. If what people say about their own experience can’t be trusted on the one thing they have direct access to, the whole inquiry has lost its anchor.

Fin
Panpsychism keeps making claims about consciousness that consciousness itself doesn’t underwrite. It posits experience where first-person reports deny it — in dreamless sleep, in atoms, in moments too brief for us to consciously notice. It asks acceptance of a “bare subjectivity” introspection never finds. It posits a fundamental experience-like layer doing no work explaining any actual feature of any actual experience.

Back to the fan. The breeze isn’t the fan. It isn’t a substance added to the fan. It’s an emergent phenomenon of the fan at work. Same with consciousness. Not a hidden ingredient added to processing. Something that emerges from the work being done. Feeling, I think, is somehow deeply linked to information processing. Something like: what it is, from the inside, for a particular sort of information processing. When this sort the processing stops, the breeze stops. No leftover breeze hiding somewhere else. Said another way: Consciousness seems tied to information processing, and if there is no information to process, there is nothing to be conscious of, and no consciousness emerges.

I’ll be honest about what this view doesn’t do. It doesn’t solve the hard problem. Why does any processing feel like anything at all? I don’t have a derivation. But panpsychism doesn’t solve it either. Positing fundamental experience and then needing it to combine into human consciousness doesn’t answer the question — it just gives the mystery a new address. The choice between them is about which unsolved mystery you’d rather hold.

And it's not really about parsimony, in the end. The deeper point is evidential. Panpsychism asks me to believe in a hidden experiential layer that I cannot find in experience, cannot test from the outside, and do not need in order to explain the actual shape of consciousness. It keeps having to override the only methodological discipline available to an inquiry into consciousness: take first-person experience seriously, because it's all you have. Once you make firsthand experience load-bearing the whole way through, the view eats itself.

I think it’s strong, readable, and philosophically alive. The best part is that it does not just say “panpsychism is weird.” It attacks the thing that makes panpsychism attractive: the appeal to first-person experience. Your core move is:

Panpsychism says consciousness is fundamental because first-person experience is the most certain thing we know. But then it posits forms of consciousness that first-person experience gives us no reason to believe in.

That is a good critique. The essay’s strongest line of attack is evidential, not metaphysical: panpsychism may be possible, but it has not earned belief.

The fan analogy works well too. It makes your emergentist picture intuitive: consciousness is not a hidden ingredient inside the parts; it is what happens when the system is doing the right kind of work. That gives the piece a clear positive alternative rather than only a rejection.

Where I think the argument is most vulnerable: you lean heavily on introspection as the “only anchor,” but a panpsychist could say introspection tells us that consciousness exists, not what its ultimate metaphysical structure must be. In other words, they might accept that we never experience “bare subjectivity” by itself while still arguing that subjectivity is an inferred ground of structured experience, the way physics infers unobservable entities from explanatory need.

The dreamless sleep section is rhetorically effective, but philosophically riskier. “I do not remember anything” and “there was nothing it was like” are very close, but not identical. A panpsychist, Buddhist philosopher, or some consciousness researchers might press you there. The stronger version of your point is not “dreamless sleep proves there is no consciousness,” but “the panpsychist has no independent warrant for saying there is consciousness there.”

The time-scale argument is interesting, but I would sharpen it. A panpsychist does not need to say you consciously experience the sub-100ms flash. They can say micro-experiential events exist below the threshold of human reportable consciousness. So the argument should be framed less as “panpsychism contradicts my testimony” and more as “panpsychism multiplies hidden experiential facts that make no difference to the actual contents, timing, or structure of human consciousness.”

The biggest improvement I would make is to separate three targets:

Full panpsychism: atoms or particles are conscious.
Panprotopsychism: fundamental stuff is proto-experiential but not conscious.
Russellian monism: physics gives structure/dynamics, while consciousness reveals intrinsic nature.

Right now, the essay sometimes slides between them. That is fine for a blog post, but a careful panpsychist could object that you are attacking the cruder version while their actual view is more subtle.

Your conclusion is excellent because it admits your own view does not solve the hard problem. That gives the piece intellectual honesty. The line “panpsychism doesn’t solve it either — it just gives the mystery a new address” is probably the essay’s best sentence.

My overall take: this is a strong anti-panpsychism essay, especially as a personal philosophical statement. Its central thesis is compelling: panpsychism borrows credibility from experience, then spends that credibility on entities experience never discloses. To make it harder to dismiss, I’d tighten the distinctions between panpsychism/protopanpsychism/Russellian monism and soften claims where memory/reportability are doing more work than they can strictly bear.

That makes sense.

Thought for 24s

Here’s a tighter, more defensible version.

Working title

Overdrawing on Experience: Panpsychism and the Limits of First-Person Warrant

Other good options:

The Introspective Warrant Problem for Panpsychism

Panpsychism and the Misuse of First-Person Evidence

First-Person Evidence and Hidden Phenomenal Ontology

Revised thesis

This paper brackets non-phenomenological arguments for panpsychism, including Russellian arguments about the intrinsic nature of matter, anti-emergentist arguments, appeals to simplicity, and worries about physicalism. Its target is narrower: the common appeal to first-person experience as privileged evidence for panpsychism.

I argue that this first-person route does not support the metaphysical conclusions panpsychists often draw from it. First-person experience may warrant the claim that consciousness exists, and it may disclose the character of ordinary conscious life: sensation, thought, affect, temporal flow, embodiment, attention, and self-relation. But it does not disclose micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance or transfer. These posits may be defended, if at all, by further theoretical arguments. They do not inherit the epistemic privilege of first-person experience merely by being described as “experiential.”

Panpsychism therefore faces an introspective warrant problem: the evidential foundation that gives the view much of its initial appeal does not support the theoretical structure built on top of it. The view may still be possible, but the first-person evidence is weaker than advertised.

Abstract

Panpsychism is often motivated by an appeal to the epistemic privilege of consciousness. While physical science appears to reveal matter only through its structural, relational, or dispositional profile, consciousness is known directly from the first-person point of view. This paper argues that this motivation generates a problem for panpsychism. I bracket non-phenomenological arguments for the view — including Russellian arguments about the intrinsic nature of matter, anti-emergentist arguments, and appeals to theoretical simplicity — and focus only on what first-person experience itself can warrant.

The central claim is that first-person experience discloses structured, content-involving consciousness: sensations, affects, thoughts, temporal flow, embodiment, attention, and self-relation. It does not disclose bare subjectivity, micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance between universal and local minds. Panpsychism therefore faces what I call the introspective warrant problem: the first-person evidence that establishes consciousness as a datum does not by itself license the hidden phenomenal ontology panpsychism requires.

This argument is not a refutation of panpsychism. Rather, it imposes a constraint. If panpsychists posit forms of consciousness or proto-consciousness not disclosed in first-person experience, those posits must be defended as theoretical entities and assessed by their explanatory power. They cannot simply inherit the special warrant of ordinary conscious experience. Unless panpsychism can show that its hidden phenomenal ontology explains the structure, unity, temporal grain, valence, and content of consciousness better than rival views, its appeal to first-person evidence overdraws on the very datum that makes the view attractive.

Short abstract

Panpsychism often appeals to the fact that consciousness is known directly from the first-person point of view. I argue that this appeal supports less than panpsychists require. First-person experience discloses structured, contentful consciousness: sensation, affect, thought, temporal flow, attention, and self-relation. It does not disclose micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance. These posits may be defended by further theoretical arguments, but they do not inherit the epistemic privilege of ordinary experience merely by being called “experiential.” Panpsychism therefore faces an introspective warrant problem: its first-person evidential foundation does not support the hidden phenomenal ontology built upon it.

Core argument
Panpsychism is often motivated by the claim that consciousness is known directly in first-person experience.
Direct first-person experience warrants only what is actually disclosed in first-person experience.
What is disclosed in first-person experience is structured, content-involving consciousness: pains, colors, thoughts, moods, bodily feelings, temporal flow, attention, memory, and self-relation.
First-person experience does not disclose bare subjectivity, micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance.
Therefore, these panpsychist posits cannot inherit the epistemic privilege of first-person consciousness.
They may still be defended by other arguments, but then their warrant is theoretical, not first-personal.
Therefore, panpsychism’s appeal to first-person experience is weaker than advertised.
Unless panpsychism provides independent explanatory payoff, its hidden phenomenal ontology remains under-motivated.
The key distinction

This is important. The paper should not say:

First-person experience does not reveal micro-experience, therefore micro-experience does not exist.

That is too strong.

The paper should say:

First-person experience does not reveal micro-experience, therefore micro-experience cannot claim the special epistemic authority of first-person experience. It must be defended as a theoretical posit.

That distinction makes the argument much harder to dismiss.

Proposed outline
I. Introduction: Panpsychism’s first-person appeal

Open by granting the appeal of panpsychism.

Panpsychism takes consciousness seriously. It refuses to treat experience as an inexplicable latecomer in a wholly non-experiential universe. It is especially attractive because consciousness seems uniquely known from within. We know our own experience directly, while we know physical reality largely through structure, behavior, relation, and causal role.

Then introduce the problem:

But if first-person experience is the evidential foundation, then panpsychism must respect the limits of what first-person experience actually gives. It cannot move freely from ordinary conscious experience to micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic consciousness, or hidden phenomenal fields.

End the introduction with the thesis.

II. Bracketing other arguments

This section protects the paper from obvious objections.

Say explicitly:

I am not arguing that panpsychism has no possible support. Nor am I addressing every argument for the view. Panpsychists may appeal to Russellian monism, the intrinsic nature of matter, the explanatory gap, the alleged impossibility of radical emergence, continuity in nature, or theoretical simplicity. I bracket those arguments here.

Then state the target:

My concern is only with the first-person route to panpsychism: the claim that consciousness, because it is known directly from within, provides special warrant for placing consciousness or proto-consciousness in fundamental reality.

This narrowing is crucial.

III. The Datum Constraint

Introduce your main principle:

The Datum Constraint: A theory that motivates itself by appeal to first-person experience may claim first-person warrant only for what first-person experience actually discloses.

You can connect this to Husserl’s “principle of all principles”: intuition gives warrant, but only within the limits of how the thing is given. The point is not that only directly experienced things exist. The point is that direct experience cannot be used as a blank check for claims that go beyond what is directly given.

Applied to panpsychism:

First-person experience warrants the existence of consciousness. It does not, by itself, warrant the claim that consciousness is fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or proto-phenomenal.

IV. What first-person experience actually discloses

This section develops your phenomenological claim.

First-person experience does not present consciousness as bare subjectivity floating free from all structure. It presents concrete conscious episodes: seeing red, feeling pain, hearing sound, remembering something, anticipating something, being anxious, feeling embodied, attending, thinking, drifting, imagining.

Your original essay makes this point well: what we know firsthand is not bare subjectivity but structured experience. Consciousness shows up as warmth, time passing, thought taking shape, identity, and other determinate features.

So:

The datum is not “mere subjectivity.” The datum is subjectivity-with-character.

This gives you your next claim:

If panpsychism begins from the datum of experience, then it must begin from structured, contentful experience. But structured experience is exactly the sort of thing that seems to require explanation in terms of organization, integration, memory, attention, and processing.

V. Bare subjectivity is an abstraction, not a datum

Here you attack “pure subjectivity.”

Argument:

Consciousness is always encountered with phenomenal character.
Even allegedly objectless states have some character: calm, anxiety, blankness, silence, temporal duration, bodily absence, mineness, clarity, opacity, intensity.
Therefore, “bare subjectivity” stripped of all phenomenal character is not something directly disclosed.
It is an abstraction from experience.
Therefore, it cannot inherit the full epistemic force of first-person acquaintance.

Careful wording:

The claim is not that every conscious state must represent an external object. Rather, every conscious state we can identify has some phenomenal structure. A subjectivity with no structure, no content, no valence, no temporality, no mineness, and no character is not something found in experience.

This is one of the strongest parts of the paper.

VI. Proto-experience and the warrant-transfer problem

Now turn to panprotopsychism.

The panprotopsychist says:

Fundamental reality is not conscious, but proto-conscious or proto-experiential.

Your reply:

But proto-experience is not given in first-person experience. It is not something we encounter from within. It is introduced as a theoretical posit to explain consciousness.

So the question becomes:

How does warrant transfer from familiar consciousness to unfamiliar proto-consciousness?

This gives you a second named problem:

The Warrant-Transfer Problem: The less a posit resembles the consciousness disclosed in first-person experience, the less it can claim support from first-person acquaintance with consciousness.

Then:

If proto-experience is sufficiently like experience, it inherits the problems of explaining structure, unity, subjecthood, and combination. If it is sufficiently unlike experience, it loses its claim to first-person warrant.

That is a nice dilemma.

VII. Goff and the escalation of metaphysical machinery

This section applies the argument to sophisticated panpsychism.

Grant that Goff-style views are not crude “atoms have tiny minds” theories. Russellian panpsychism claims that consciousness or proto-consciousness may constitute the intrinsic nature of physical reality. Cosmopsychism tries to avoid the combination problem by beginning with a cosmic subject rather than micro-subjects. Hybrid cosmopsychist views then introduce further machinery: local subjects, cosmic subjectivity, phenomenal inheritance, qualia transfer, or subject-grounding relations.

Then your objection:

The more sophisticated the view becomes, the more severe the introspective warrant problem becomes. Micro-experience was already not disclosed in first-person experience. But cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, and qualia-transfer relations are even farther removed from anything first-person experience gives.

Key sentence:

The more panpsychism develops the machinery needed to solve its internal problems, the farther it moves from the first-person datum that originally motivated it.

Another strong sentence:

Goff-style world-building may be metaphysically ingenious, but it cannot claim the epistemic immediacy of ordinary conscious experience.

VIII. Hidden posits are allowed — but they need explanatory payoff

This section prevents the paper from becoming crude empiricism.

You should say:

The objection is not that unobservable entities are illegitimate. Electrons, fields, black holes, genes, and quarks are not directly given in experience either. But they earn warrant through explanatory, predictive, and unificatory work.

Then apply that standard:

Once micro-experience or cosmic subjectivity is admitted to be a theoretical posit rather than a first-person datum, it must be evaluated like other theoretical posits. What does it explain? What does it predict? What phenomena would be less intelligible without it?

Then list the features that need explaining:

unity of consciousness;
subjecthood;
phenomenal content;
temporal structure;
valence;
attention;
memory;
selfhood;
sleep and anesthesia;
brain damage;
development;
the difference between conscious and unconscious processing.

Your claim:

Panpsychism does not explain these features merely by placing experience at the fundamental level. In many cases, the explanatory work is still done by organization, information processing, and system-level structure.

This connects directly to your “decorative layer” worry from the essay.

IX. Sleep and temporal grain as case studies

Use these briefly, not as the paper’s foundation.

Dreamless sleep

Do not claim too much.

Bad version:

Dreamless sleep proves consciousness stops.

Better version:

Dreamless sleep illustrates the danger of positing hidden experience where first-person evidence is absent or ambiguous. The panpsychist may say there was experience without memory, but this requires independent argument. It is not licensed by first-person report.

Temporal grain

Again, make it modest.

Human consciousness appears to have thresholds, integration windows, and a temporal grain. Very brief stimuli may be processed without entering conscious experience. If panpsychism responds by positing hidden micro-experiential facts beneath reportable consciousness, those facts do not explain why consciousness has the temporal structure it has.

These cases show the general pattern:

Panpsychism repeatedly posits experience where experience itself gives no clear warrant.

X. Conclusion

Return to the main point:

Panpsychism is not refuted. But its first-person motivation is disciplined. First-person experience establishes that consciousness exists and discloses some of its ordinary features. It does not establish that consciousness is fundamental, microphysical, cosmic, or proto-phenomenal.

Then finish with:

If panpsychism is to be defended, it must be defended as a speculative theoretical account with independent explanatory virtues. It cannot simply borrow the authority of first-person consciousness and spend it on hidden phenomenal metaphysics.

Notes for drafting
Best phrase to keep

Panpsychism overdraws on first-person evidence.

That captures the paper beautifully.

Best technical term

Introspective warrant problem

Definition:

The problem that first-person experience warrants ordinary consciousness but does not warrant the hidden phenomenal posits panpsychism requires.

Best principle

The Datum Constraint

First-person evidence has authority only within the limits of what first-person experience actually discloses.

Best argumentative move

Separate these three things:

The datum: ordinary conscious experience exists.
The characterization: ordinary conscious experience is structured, contentful, temporal, affective, embodied, and self-involving.
The metaphysical inflation: consciousness, proto-consciousness, or subjectivity exists fundamentally, microphysically, cosmically, or universally.

Your argument is that panpsychists move too quickly from 1 to 3 while neglecting 2.

Best objection you will face

A panpsychist will say:

We are not claiming first-person experience directly reveals micro-experience. We are making an inference to the best explanation.

Your reply:

Good. Then the argument is no longer grounded in direct first-person warrant. It is a theoretical inference and must be evaluated by explanatory standards. The question becomes whether micro-experience, proto-experience, or cosmic subjectivity actually explains ordinary consciousness better than rival accounts.

That is exactly where you want the debate.

Strongest final formulation

First-person experience gives panpsychism a datum, not a metaphysics. It establishes that consciousness exists, but it does not establish that consciousness is fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or proto-phenomenal. Those are theoretical claims. They may be true, but they do not inherit the epistemic privilege of the datum from which panpsychism begins.

That is the core of the paper.

This is a competent alternative — it has some genuinely good moves we should steal, and some that pull teeth out of your argument and should be left alone. Let me name both.

**What to steal:**

*The bracketing section.* This is the single best move in the document. Saying explicitly "I am not attacking Russellian arguments about intrinsic nature, anti-emergentism, or appeals to simplicity — only the first-person route" cordons off a huge class of objections and lets the paper be the kind of paper journal referees actually accept: narrow, defensible, surgical. Add a Section II like this. It also reframes the thesis as targeted rather than imperialistic, which matters for academic reception.

*The three-step distinction.* Datum / characterization / metaphysical inflation. Clean, memorable, and it gives you a precise way to say what panpsychists are doing wrong — collapsing the gap between step 1 and step 3 while skipping over what step 2 actually shows. Worth lifting almost verbatim into the paper's framing.

*The modest framing on sleep and temporal grain.* The earlier outline had them shouldering more weight than they can carry. Demoting them to case studies that *illustrate the constraint* rather than independent objections that defeat panpsychism makes them sharper, not weaker. You can still keep the fork-structure of your sleep argument and the explanatory-impotence point of the time argument — just frame each as one instance of the general pattern.

**What to leave alone:**

*The retreat from the structure/dynamics argument.* Section VII of this version gestures at Goff's sophistication but never engages the actual argument. That's a problem. If you bracket Russellian arguments entirely, a reviewer will say: "But Goff's case doesn't really rest on first-person privilege — it rests on structure/dynamics. You've attacked a strawman." You need a brief engagement showing that even granting structure/dynamics, the constraint still applies — because structure/dynamics shows physics is dispositional, not that *phenomenal* properties fill the categorical role. That's a separate inferential step, and the constraint applies along it.

*Two named principles.* The document introduces both the "Datum Constraint" and the "Warrant-Transfer Problem" as distinct named things. That's one too many — they're the same constraint at different levels of abstraction. Pick one. I'd keep "introspective warrant constraint" as the named principle and let "warrant-transfer" or "overdraft" be the descriptive way you talk about its violations.

*The Husserl connection.* "Principle of all principles" is a real Husserlian doctrine and invoking it borrows pedigree, but it also commits you to a phenomenological framing the paper doesn't need. If you're writing for analytic philosophy of mind, Husserl will feel out of place; if you're writing for phenomenology, your targets (Goff, Chalmers, Strawson) will feel out of place. Pick a tradition. My read is your argument lives in the analytic register — keep it there and skip Husserl.

Working title

Overdrawing on Experience: Panpsychism and the Introspective Warrant Constraint

Alternate:

First-Person Evidence and the Hidden Ontology of Panpsychism

Core thesis

Panpsychism often begins from an epistemic privilege claim: consciousness is known directly from the first-person point of view. But it then uses that privilege to support claims about micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or phenomenal inheritance relations — none of which are themselves disclosed in first-person experience.

The problem is not that these posits are impossible. The problem is that they cannot inherit the special epistemic authority of first-person experience. If they are to be defended, they must be defended as theoretical posits. And once assessed as theoretical posits, they must earn their keep by explaining the actual features of consciousness: unity, content, temporal structure, valence, attention, selfhood, sleep, anesthesia, and the distinction between conscious and unconscious processing.

So the result is stronger than mere “discipline”:

Panpsychism may remain possible, but the special epistemic standing it advertises from first-person experience is forfeit wherever its metaphysics outruns what first-person experience discloses.

Revised abstract

Panpsychism often derives its appeal from an epistemic asymmetry. Consciousness, unlike the entities posited by physical science, is known directly from the first-person point of view. This appeal gives panpsychism much of its intuitive force: if consciousness is the one reality we know from within, perhaps it belongs in the fundamental ontology. This paper argues that this first-person motivation supports less than panpsychists often require.

I introduce the introspective warrant constraint: a theory that motivates itself by appeal to first-person experience may claim first-person warrant only for what first-person experience actually discloses. First-person experience discloses structured, content-involving consciousness — sensations, affects, thoughts, temporal flow, embodiment, attention, memory, and self-relation. It does not disclose micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance between universal and local minds. These may be possible theoretical posits, but they do not inherit the epistemic privilege of ordinary conscious experience merely by being described as “phenomenal” or “experiential.”

The paper brackets independent arguments for panpsychism, including anti-emergentist arguments, appeals to simplicity, and general dissatisfaction with physicalism. It focuses instead on the first-person route to panpsychism. I argue that even Russellian structure/dynamics arguments do not escape the constraint: showing that physics gives only structural or dispositional knowledge does not show that the categorical nature of matter is phenomenal. That is a further inferential step, not something disclosed by first-person experience. The conclusion is not that panpsychism is refuted, but that its advertised first-person warrant is overextended. First-person experience gives panpsychism a datum; it does not give it a hidden phenomenal ontology.

The three-step distinction

This should become one of the paper’s framing devices.

1. The datum

Consciousness exists. There is something it is like to undergo experience.

This is the part panpsychism is entitled to take from first-person experience.

2. The characterization

What first-person experience actually discloses is not bare subjectivity, but structured experience: pain, color, warmth, thought, attention, memory, time, affect, embodiment, self-relation, etc. Your original essay makes this point clearly: what we know firsthand is not “pure mere-feeling,” but structured, contentful consciousness.

3. The metaphysical inflation

Panpsychism then moves from the datum of consciousness to claims about consciousness or proto-consciousness as fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or hidden in the intrinsic nature of matter.

Your objection is that panpsychists too often move from 1 to 3 while not taking 2 seriously enough.

That is the paper’s central move.

But if first-person experience is the source of panpsychism’s special warrant, then panpsychism must respect the limits of what first-person experience actually gives.

First-person experience warrants consciousness as a datum. It does not warrant panpsychism’s hidden phenomenal ontology.

Subjectivity is always encountered with character. A subjectivity stripped of all content, valence, temporality, mineness, intensity, and structure is not something found in experience. It is an abstraction from experience.

This is where your “you need something to feel” argument belongs.

V. The failure of warrant transfer

Now apply the constraint.

Panpsychists want to move from ordinary first-person consciousness to:

micro-experience;
proto-experience;
proto-phenomenal properties;
cosmic subjectivity;
hidden phenomenal fields;
phenomenal inheritance or qualia-transfer relations.

But none of these is directly disclosed in ordinary first-person experience.

So:

The panpsychist cannot simply borrow the epistemic privilege of consciousness and spend it on entities that consciousness itself never reveals.

This is the “overdraft” idea.

VI. Structure/dynamics and the categorical nature move

Even if physics gives only structure and dynamics, it does not follow that the categorical nature underlying that structure is phenomenal. VII. Goff-style world-building

Now apply this to sophisticated panpsychism.

Goff’s move toward cosmopsychism or hybrid cosmopsychism is not crude particle-consciousness. It is designed to avoid the combination problem. That does not show Goff is wrong. It shows that his view cannot retain the rhetorical force of direct first-person evidence. It becomes speculative metaphysics.

VIII. Sleep and temporal grain as case studies

Demote these from main arguments to illustrations.

Dreamless sleep

Use the modest but still sharp version:

Dreamless sleep does not prove that consciousness is absent. But it illustrates the problem of positing hidden experience where first-person evidence is absent or ambiguous. The claim that there was experience with no memory, no report, and no phenomenological trace requires independent warrant.

Temporal grain

Consciousness appears to have thresholds and integration windows. Very brief stimuli may be processed without entering experience. If panpsychism responds by assigning hidden experientiality below the threshold of consciousness, it has not explained conscious temporal structure; it has merely multiplied hidden phenomenal facts beneath it.

These examples support the general pattern from your essay: panpsychism posits experience where experience itself gives no clear warrant.

IX. Explanatory burden

Once panpsychist posits are treated as theoretical, the question becomes:

What do they explain?

Do they explain:

why consciousness is unified?
why it has contents?
why it has a temporal grain?
why it is altered by sleep, anesthesia, attention, memory, trauma, brain damage?
why some processing is conscious and other processing is not?
why there is one subject rather than many?
why experience has valence?

Your pressure point:

If the actual structure of consciousness is explained, if at all, by organization and information processing, then the hidden phenomenal layer looks explanatorily idle.

That is the academic version of your fan/breeze analogy.

Panpsychism is not disproved. But its first-person motivation is significantly weakened. First-person experience establishes that consciousness exists; it does not establish that consciousness is fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or proto-phenomenal.

Panpsychism overdraws on experience when it treats the epistemic privilege of ordinary consciousness as support for a hidden phenomenal ontology. Those further posits may be possible, but they do not have the warrant panpsychism often implies they have.

Draft introduction

Panpsychism derives much of its contemporary appeal from an epistemic asymmetry. Consciousness, unlike the entities posited by physical science, is known directly from the first-person point of view. I do not infer that I am in pain in the way I infer the presence of an electron, a gene, or a gravitational wave. I undergo the pain. I live the experience from within. By contrast, physical science seems to describe matter in terms of structure, relation, disposition, and causal role. It tells us what matter does, but not, at least on one influential line of thought, what matter is intrinsically. If consciousness is the one reality known from the inside, then perhaps consciousness — or something consciousness-like — belongs in our account of fundamental reality.

This thought gives panpsychism its distinctive force. It promises to avoid the apparent miracle of consciousness emerging from a wholly non-conscious base by locating mentality, or proto-mentality, in the nature of matter itself. But the appeal to first-person experience also imposes a constraint. If first-person experience is the source of panpsychism’s special warrant, then the view may claim that warrant only for what first-person experience actually discloses. And what first-person experience discloses is not micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance between universal and local minds. It discloses ordinary, structured, content-involving consciousness: sensation, affect, thought, attention, temporal flow, embodiment, memory, and self-relation.

This paper argues that panpsychism frequently overdraws on the evidential authority of first-person experience. I call the relevant principle the introspective warrant constraint: first-person evidence has authority only within the limits of what first-person experience discloses. The constraint does not imply that unobserved or unobservable entities are illegitimate. It does not say that micro-experience, proto-experience, or cosmic subjectivity is impossible. Rather, it says that such posits cannot inherit the epistemic privilege of ordinary consciousness merely by being described as “phenomenal” or “experiential.” If they are not disclosed in first-person experience, they must be defended as theoretical posits and assessed by their explanatory power.

My target is therefore limited but significant. I do not argue here against every motivation for panpsychism. I bracket anti-emergentist arguments, appeals to simplicity, and broad dissatisfaction with physicalism. I also bracket the full Russellian case that physics reveals only the structural or dispositional nature of matter. That step is not licensed by first-person experience alone. The structure/dynamics argument may create a vacancy in our metaphysics; it does not show that consciousness fills it.

The conclusion is not that panpsychism is refuted. It is that one of panpsychism’s most powerful sources of philosophical appeal cannot bear the weight placed on it. First-person experience gives panpsychism a datum, not a metaphysics. It establishes that consciousness exists and discloses certain features of ordinary conscious life. It does not establish that consciousness is fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or hidden in the categorical basis of physical structure. If panpsychism is to be defended, it must earn those claims by independent explanatory work. Until then, its hidden phenomenal ontology does not possess the special warrant that the datum of consciousness itself enjoys.

Draft Section III: The Introspective Warrant Constraint

Panpsychism often draws its force from a claim about the special epistemic status of consciousness. Consciousness is not known in the way electrons, genes, or black holes are known. I do not infer my current pain from its behavioral effects, nor do I posit my visual experience as a theoretical entity. I undergo it. Conscious experience is given from the first-person point of view.

This appeal is legitimate as far as it goes. But it does not go as far as panpsychists often need it to go. The fact that consciousness is known directly does not mean that every entity described as “phenomenal,” “proto-phenomenal,” or “experiential” inherits the warrant of first-person experience. First-person warrant is not a transferable credit. It attaches to what is actually given in experience.

Call this the introspective warrant constraint:

A theory that motivates itself by appeal to first-person experience may claim first-person warrant only for what first-person experience actually discloses.

The constraint is modest. It does not say that only directly experienced entities exist. It does not prohibit theoretical posits. Physics, biology, and psychology all legitimately posit entities and structures not given in ordinary experience. But those posits are warranted by explanatory, predictive, or unificatory work. They do not inherit the authority of first-person acquaintance. If panpsychism posits micro-experience, proto-experience, cosmic subjectivity, hidden phenomenal fields, or relations of phenomenal inheritance, those posits may be defended as theoretical entities. They may not be treated as if they were directly warranted by the first-person datum of consciousness.

The point is easiest to see by distinguishing three steps. First, there is the datum: consciousness exists. There is something it is like to undergo pain, see red, hear a sound, remember a face, or feel time passing. Second, there is the characterization of the datum: first-person experience presents consciousness as structured, determinate, temporal, affective, embodied, attentive, and often self-involving. Third, there is metaphysical inflation: the claim that consciousness, proto-consciousness, or subjectivity belongs to the fundamental nature of matter, exists at the microphysical level, or characterizes the universe as a whole.

The introspective warrant constraint allows the first step. It also allows the second. But it blocks any automatic passage to the third. First-person experience warrants the existence of ordinary consciousness and discloses something about its character. It does not, by itself, disclose consciousness as fundamental, ubiquitous, microphysical, cosmic, or hidden in the categorical basis of physical reality.

This does not show that panpsychism is false. It shows that a particular route to panpsychism is weaker than advertised. If the panpsychist says that microphysical entities are conscious, or that fundamental properties are proto-experiential, or that the universe is a conscious subject, those claims require further argument. They cannot be licensed simply by pointing to the immediacy of ordinary experience. The immediacy of pain warrants the pain. It does not warrant a cosmic subject.

This constraint matters especially because panpsychism often presents itself as unusually faithful to consciousness. Its promise is to take experience seriously rather than explaining it away. But taking experience seriously also means taking seriously the limits of what experience gives. First-person experience gives us conscious life as lived: sensations, affects, thoughts, moods, temporal flow, attention, memory, embodiment, and self-relation. It does not give us bare subjectivity detached from all phenomenal character, nor micro-experiences hidden in particles, nor proto-experiential categorical properties, nor a unified cosmic experiencer.

Once this is recognized, the dialectic changes. The issue is no longer whether consciousness is real. It is. The issue is whether panpsychism’s hidden phenomenal ontology is supported by the same first-person evidence that supports the reality of consciousness. The answer is no. That ontology may still be defended, but only as theory. It must earn its keep.
