Andersen Department of Philosophy, Simon Fraser University Burnaby, British Columbia, Canada handerse@sfu.ca  ORCID 0000-0002-7950-8186  Abstract: Realist metaphysical views often rely, explicitly or implicitly, on variations of the presupposition that genuine structure is sparse; call this assumption Sparsity. This includes views where there is one uniquely correct way to carve up the world, and also apparently pluralistic views that allow more structure yet still add a limit so there is not 'too' much. This conﬂates the question of how to characterize what structure is, with two other questions: how much structure there is; and which particular structures there are. I draw on Dennettian patterns, combining deﬂationary realism with proﬂigacy about real patterns, to demonstrate a pragmatist metaphysical approach to identifying structure without begging the question of how much structure there is. Pragmatist pattern metaphysics allows that the world may be dense in structure, beyond any presupposed particular upper bound from Sparsity. Finding genuine structure may be easy, and most of it reiterative, useless, or uninteresting; the world may be so dense with structure that we could never exhaust it, such that trying to ﬁnd 'the' correct structure is in principle unachievable. This shifts the substantive task away from the metaphysical question of what structure is, and towards methodological considerations. If there may be more genuine structure than we could ever investigate, our interests as inquirers must play a role in narrowing down which structures we choose to investigate, and which of many closely related structures yields the most systematicity, usefulness, or other epistemic desiderata. This pragmatist approach is minimalist with respect to metaphysical commitment: saying some structure is genuine says very little of substance about it.    1. Introduction There is a common presupposition buried in the discussion among philosophers of science and metaphysicians, made by even those who appear to otherwise starkly disagree, about how much genuine structure the world contains. Call this assumption the Sparsity of Structure: that there is structure in the world, but only so much of it, and structure is suﬀiciently sparse that some or most of what appears like structure is instead merely apparent and not genuine. This is often used as part of an initial set of constraints on what we are looking for in a good account of what structure is, or how to distinguish 'real' from merely apparent structure. This paper will disentangle two metaphysical questions that require diﬀerent resources to answer, and provide a pragmatist metaphysical answer to each.  This pragmatist approach relies on the inextricable interconnections between metaphysical constraints or principles on one hand, and methodological norms and guidance on the other. It should not be the job of our metaphysical criteria for structure to decide in advance how rich the

I will oﬀer a brief argument in favour of the possibility that the world may be so dense with structure that it is inexhaustible by description; this highlights the potential danger in presupposing any degree of sparsity in order to yield the conclusion that some apparent structure is spurious or merely apparent. Even if one does not want to take a pragmatist approach to metaphysics, unearthing and rejecting the Sparsity of Structure presupposition is still useful, as it illustrates how a substantive empirical assumption can be baked in as metaphysics. A modiﬁed version of Dennett's pattern realism provides a pragmatist metaphysical foundation for identifying structure. When using pragmatist pattern ontologies without the Sparsity assumption, we end up with more identiﬁable structure in the world than allowed by other authors who rely on patterns, such as Ladyman, Ross and Collier, and yet with more actual structure, rather than noise, than the version of patterns oﬀered by McAllister. The use of 'pattern' and pattern ontologies as an account of what structure is provides a minimal metaphysical basis from which to proceed to empirical questions like which structures there are. Pattern metaphysics alone yields vast quantities of pattern structure that could be deﬁned, some of which may not be identiﬁable in any part of the world; it also yields a surfeit of genuine structure because there is a vast array of ways in which any given pattern identiﬁcation conditions could be slightly re-described, and patterns are deﬁned by their identiﬁcation conditions. Shifting the identiﬁcation conditions, including noise tolerance, for a given pattern results in a new pattern; if the ﬁrst had identiﬁable instances in the world, the next will very likely also have some. All of those variations can be picking out genuine structure. The world may be dense with such structure. Using Dennettian patterns means that all of those are equally real, on an extremely minimal sense of realism. In this regard, the pragmatist metaphysical picture shares a surprising amount with Lewis' point about being a realist about possible worlds. Lewis oﬀered realism about one kind of thing, namely, worlds. There are a lot of worlds, but only one metaphysical kind, worlds. Here, the pragmatist metaphysician just needs the Dennettian 'pattern'. Then, there are staggering large numbers of possible patterns, and a smaller but still rather staggering amount of those possible patterns that can be used to identify actual instances in the world. There are lots of patterns, but metaphysically, just 'pattern.' This moves the substantive work done away from the purely metaphysical, and into the methodological, including the sciences. This remains metaphysical because we don't have to use every possible variation of any pattern; we use further considerations, such as cohesion with other patterns in an ontology, and coherence among use across diﬀerent ontologies (e.g. Andersen 2026) Most of the work in picking out structure is not ﬁnding all the structure that could be found, but rather, ﬁnding the best version of those variations, where 'best' is given by further conditions that are not metaphysical.   2. Disentangling two questions about structure A lot of ink has been spilled in recent decades about how to ﬁnd structure, and how to distinguish structure that is better in some way - more real, more fundamental, more natural, more scientiﬁc, more causally eﬀicacious - from merely apparent but un-genuine structure. Answers have ranged

widely over what structure is, and, accordingly, how we ought to ﬁnd such structure, especially how to discern the genuine structure from the spurious or merely apparent. There have been, along with these discussions, further discussion about how much structure there is, though these have tended towards the sweeping generalizations of "not very much, that's why it's special" to "a lot, if we ﬁnd it this way" to "even more than you thought" styles of pluralism. But, as I will argue in this and the next section, many of these views share an underlying assumption about how much structure there is, namely, that there is some upper bound on it such that there isn't too much (for diﬀerent understandings of too much) and thus it is a central metaphysical task to sort it into real vs derivative or variations thereon.  A quick caveat about terminology. I will aim to present an extremely broad and inclusive understanding of realism, as holding that there is anything genuinely ‘out there’ such that we are getting at any of it with anything in the sciences. I will be using the term ‘structure’ for this, in a Sellarsian broadest possible sense of that term. Structure is useful for a couple reasons. It covers a lot of more speciﬁc projects, such as those of ﬁnding causal structure, investigating social structures, realism about mathematical structures, and more. The word structure does not have to carry any particular signiﬁcance, other than that there is something ‘out there’ that our models, theories, discourses, and other representational devices may or may not succeed in picking out.1 I will also be relying on a pattern metaphysics, with patterns and pattern ontologies, as an account of what structure is; on this approach, other potential speciﬁc views of structure, such as entities or invariant mathematical representations, are special cases of patterns, not competitor alternatives. The patterns metaphysics is helpful for illustration but the main concerns about the Sparsity assumption, its consequences, and what it looks like to reject Sparsity, still stand even if one does not want to adopt a pattern-based or other kind of pragmatist metaphysics. The ﬁrst question to disentangle is that of what makes any particular structure real, such that an answer to it will tell us what is 'really' real, from that of how much real structure there is. There are many varieties of realism that answer this ﬁrst question. Answers to this also include accounts of kindhood where some kinds are natural and some are not (though, some accounts of natural kinds also diminish the importance of that boundary; inter alia, Chakravartty 2023, Khalidi 2018). It includes accounts that otherwise diﬀer widely, such as Sider's (2013) reference magnetism where claims about the physical world are made true by snapping like magnets onto the closest uniquely real joints in the world, alongside weak accounts of realism where to be is to be the value of a bound variable (e.g. Quine 1948). It also includes accounts where the question of what is real is left behind in favour of questions about comparative fundamentality (e.g. Schaﬀer 2009), since this still requires sorting apparent structure into uniquely correct orderings with respect to fundamentality, and still presupposes a high degree of sparsity to what is 'really' fundamental.

Philosophers of science have developed accounts to answer the question of what makes any particular structure real that start from the assumption that our best current scientiﬁc theories or models involve real structure, and then 'read oﬀ' from those theories what there is. Outsourcing this question to the sciences to determine what structure is genuine is called naturalizing metaphysics (inter alia, French 2014; see Guay and Pradeu 2020 for discussion and critical remarks on this). Various ﬂavours of structural realism construe structure as continuous through theory change; stronger versions such as ontic structural realism also include some forward-looking goal of structure to which the sciences will eventually approaches (e.g. French 2006, French and Ladyman 2010, Ladyman Ross Spurrett and Collier 2007).  Some but not all of these answers to the question of what there is presuppose uniqueness of structure. Uniqueness means there is one uniquely right way to carve up the joints of the world, and our job is to ﬁgure out what that is, such that our metaphysical analysis is the technique for correct carving and it delivers the correct answer when applied to the world (and merely epistemic limitations might stand in the way). This applies to all the varieties of answers, not just realism: views about fundamentality or grounding also often presuppose that there is one uniquely correct ranking of comparative fundamentality. Some answers, though, don't require the presupposition of uniquess, no single best way of carving up structure. Cartwright’s dappled world (1999) is a realist view that accommodates a wide plurality of structure; similarly, rainforest realism, and other similarly named realisms (e.g. Dupre 1981). In these cases, there can be more than one way of carving things up, even for the same set of phenomena, and a plurality of ontologies can co-exist.  Answering this ﬁrst question, about what there is, involves some non-zero level of realist commitment but can vary with respect to how unique assumed results of applying that to the world will be. In addition to varying with respect to uniqueness of structure, these views can vary along a second parameter in terms of how much strength is involved in the realism itself. The strength of realism, this second parameter, can be extremely robust, such as the knee-jerk realism of Sider (2013). It could also be extremely minimal, such as Dennett’s pattern realism (1991). Weak realism can allow for a larger range of possibility for how much genuine structure there could turn out to be, because there is much less involved in committing to the reality of such structure. If saying, yes this is genuine structure, carries comparatively little import one can allow in more structure. If it is a very weighty and serious commitment, one might be more selective in what gets to count as real. In this regard, uniqueness of structure and strength of realism are part of answering the question of how to identify genuine structure, or, to diﬀerentiate genuine from merely apparent structure. The ﬁrst question, what makes any particular structure real, can thus vary along at least two parameters, the uniqueness of ways of carving up the world with that analysis, and the strength of realism attributed to the results of applying it. The second question to disentangle, then, is that of how much structure there is. This second question, how much structure there is, is easily conﬂated with the ﬁrst question, what is structure, because the second question is closely connected to, though not the same as, this second parameter along which the ﬁrst question varies. If one increases the weightiness assigned to anything that is real, on a given account of realism, then this might lead one

to (though it is not necessitated to) presuppose that, because it is so weighty to call structure real, there oughtn't be much of it.  It may be that we live in a world that has very little genuine structure (though, in my view, there is already suﬀicient evidence to tell strongly against that). It may be that we live in a world that has lots of structure, like a dappled or rainforest realism. But, as I show in the next section, even these views tend to add some upper bound to how much there could be, such that there are amounts that seem too high and are ruled out in advance. Answers to the ﬁrst question can thus implicitly presuppose some acceptable range of answer to this second question, and in doing so, presuppose an overly restrictive range. This presupposed range of acceptable quantity of structure, or, answers to the second question, informs answers to the ﬁrst question, which aim for a characterization of what structure is that will necessarily yield less structure when applied as criteria to empirical investigations. In presupposing some sparsity, even if it is comparatively generous, these views add a substantive and conceptually prior metaphysical presupposition, as an implicit answer to this second question. Both the spare and the proﬂigate ontologies may involve a priori metaphysical commitment that thus shapes subsequent inquiry. This expectation of comparative sparsity rules out a priori the possibility of being surprised by how much genuine structure a given view of realism might turn up when applied in the sciences. In the next sections, I'll urge that we should not presuppose that there is 'only so much' structure prior to ﬁnding out what structure there is and what the best methods are for distinguishing structure. In addition to being an unjustiﬁably strong initial assumption, Sparsity can also lead to a misconstrual of the task of developing ontology from the sciences using metaphysical criteria, in two mis-steps. One mis-step is to presuppose that there must be one uniquely best or correct ontology. The other mis-step is to construe the task of ﬁnding ‘the’ structure as one where we should be trying to ﬁnd all the structure: by ﬁnding the way to identify genuine structure, we can ﬁnd all there is to be found, and thus complete the task of diﬀerentiating structure from non-structure. It might turn out that structure is sparse, but we ought not presuppose that in advance. A streamlined metaphysics can answer the ﬁrst disentangled question and then treat the second question as empirical, not metaphysical.  3. How much structure is there? A range of answers In order to further disentangle the questions of what structure is from how much of it we should expect to ﬁnd, this section compares some speciﬁc answers to the question of how much structure there is, in order to show how the assumption of Sparsity can show up even in proﬂigately realist views. I'll start with the strongest versions of Sparsity, and then contrast three diﬀerent views that rely on patterns metaphysics of structure, showing how Sparsity shows up even with apparently inclusive accounts of realism.

One end of the spectrum with respect to sparsity of structure is the view that there is one and only one correct set of joints in the world (Uniqueness), there are not many of those special joints (Sparsity), and the goal of science is to eventually identify them all. This is common in contemporary post-Lewisian analytic metaphysics (e.g. Sider 2013, North 2021, Schaﬀer 2009). There may be more than one set of joint-like structures, such as in the grounding literature, but even if the chain of grounding goes on indeﬁnitely, there is still a unique ordering of fundamentality. It is easy to see how these views presuppose sparsity of structure. They also oﬀer a realism that weights the second parameter especially heavily: it is a very big deal, metaphysically, to label a joint in the world as genuine, so we shouldn't do it too often. Here, we can also see, from the presupposition of both Uniqueness and Sparsity, that it makes sense to then think of the task of the sciences as that of ﬁnding all the joints in the world (Totality) such that there is some Final Science that will deliver those. It is fairly common in post-Lewisian metaphysics to think it is at least possible for there to be an End Science such that the contents of that end science can be referred to, even if we don't yet know what they are. The intuition that metaphysics is not just about ﬁnding genuine structure, but also all the genuine structure, then drives the intuitions of Uniqueness and Sparsity, without which Totality would be unachievable. One might want to oppose strong views like knee-jerk realism to views like rainforest realism, oﬀered by Ross, Ladyman, and Collier (2007) (see also Ross 2000 for a diﬀerent version), which rejects both Totality and Uniqueness. They draw on Dennett's patterns with modiﬁcations to the deﬁnition of patterns. Their view of realism rejects objects as the basis for ontology, and allows in so much as real that they liken it to the rainforest in contrast to a Quinean desert landscape. Even here, though, there is an implicit reliance on some upper bound of structure such that Dennett's original view allows in too much and consequently must be modiﬁed to avoid that.  …let us return to Dennett’s account of real patterns. There are, as noted above, problems with it. Though it oﬀers the major achievement of the compressibility requirement—that a real pattern cannot be a bitmap of its elements—as a necessary condition on real-pattern-hood, Dennett never provides an analysis of suﬀicient conditions. (Obviously compressibility isn’t suﬀicient, or every redescription of anything would conjure a new pattern into being.) (p. 205) This parenthetical remark at the end conveys the implicit metaphysical assumption that there is only so much structure. They justify the modiﬁcation of the deﬁnition of structure (in this case, patterns) as required to limit the amount of structure it allows for. On Dennett's original version, a redescription of a pattern changes either the identiﬁcation conditions for it, or the noise/error tolerance, such that redescription would, in fact, pick out a mostly overlapping but non-identical pattern. This is ruled out by Ladyman, Ross, and Collier as allowing in too much structure; they attribute almost magical properties to redescription as conjuration of structure into existence (this issue of redescription of patterns will ﬁgure centrally in the next section). The metaphysical criterion by which structure is identiﬁed, the deﬁnition of patterns, is restricted in advance of using that criterion to see how structure there is. This move presupposes a much higher upper bound on the second question, how much structure there is, than views with sparse and

Consider a view on the furthest other end of this spectrum, that of McAllister (especially 2010, 2011; see also 1997). McAllister draws on patterns and the distinction between data and phenomena (Bogen and Woodward 1988). He argues that all possible patterns can be found in any data set, allowing for any given pattern to be instantiated with 100% noise. Since structure in datasets reﬂect phenomena, or, structure in the world, this means that the world exhibits all possible structures, using a noise/error tolerance up to and including 100%. Any empirical data set exhibits all possible patterns, each with a certain noise term. The magnitude and other properties of this noise term are irrelevant to the evidential status of a pattern: all patterns exhibited in empirical data constitute evidence of structures in the world. Furthermore, distinct patterns constitute evidence of distinct structures in the world. It follows that the world must be regarded as containing all possible structures. (McAllister 2009, p. 73) This handily deﬁnes the other end of the spectrum, as far from the commitment to uniqueness of structure as possible. It answers to the ﬁrst question, what structure is, and adds an empirical component to the second question, how we ascertain how much structure there is, by the use of datasets and the patterns in data as evidence for structure in the world. Rainforest realism reins in patterns as too proﬂigate, while McAllister's version goes the entirety of the other direction, allowing in all possibly deﬁnable structure as genuine. McAllister has reasons for taking this consequence on, including an emphasis that deﬁning structure doesn't conjure anything into being. For my purposes here, though, if the world is stipulated to contain all possible structures before we even investigate it, then the notion of structure in the metaphysical analysis diﬀerentiates nothing when applied to the world, thus not accomplishing the task it was meant to for realism. With knee-jerk realism and a sparse unique set of joints on one end, and all possible structures on the other, the full range of possible outcomes to the question, how much structure is there? can be arranged. Rainforest realism uses patterns, which rejects Uniqueness, but reins in how many patterns there could in principle turn out to be, thus capping the answer to this second question before empirical investigation. This is a form of the Sparsity assumption. The Density of Structure An immediate response to this proﬂigacy with respect to patterns is that this is far too much structure, much of it redundant in the sense that slight redescriptions pick out overlapping pattern instances, not genuinely independent and 'new' structure. One might object that even if one agrees that we should disentangle the conﬂated questions of what structure is, and how much of it there might be, there must be some limit to how much structure there is, and that limit must be lower than what turns out as possible on patterns metaphysics. This objection might press further: surely these two questions can be disentangled without thereby being completely separate. Answering either requires some commitment with respect to the other.  I agree that these questions, while distinct, involve one another. Once one commits to a view on what structure is, that thereby constrains how much of it could be found, and vice versa, where if one holds that there should only be 'so much and no more', then this limits what metaphysical views could be consistent with that. Nevertheless, there are at least two good reasons to leave, in the metaphysical characterization of what structure is, as wide a margin as possible for empirical answers to how much structure there is. One reason is that presupposing any upper limit to how much structure there is unnecessarily inﬂates the metaphysical by deﬂating the empirical or epistemological. This will be developed in the next section. Another reason to leave this wide margin is to avoid accidentally getting it wrong: if the world does turn out to have more structure than expected, we would be systematically prevented by our own metaphysical commitments from

This will be developed in this section, through an argument that a patterns metaphysics is both minimal and also leaves open that the world is so dense with structure that we could not exhaust it via description. Even if we deﬁned new patterns forever, and checking the world for them took no time, there are patterns with continuously varying parameters. Variations of them exist for every point on a real number line, which is uncountably inﬁnite, while our ability to deﬁne and check for patterns is somewhere below countably inﬁnite. We simply couldn't check for all possibly deﬁnable patterns. There wouldn't be any upper limit that could conceivably count as sparse.  The procedure for using pattern metaphysics for structure illuminates this. Consider pattern metaphysics with no added stipulation about how much structure there may or must be. Start with some pattern A, with determinate identiﬁcation conditions, along with a speciﬁed noise tolerance less than 100%. This means that pattern A is well deﬁned, by which I mean, one can pick up some chunk of the world and ask of it, does pattern A occur here? and be able to determinately return a yes or no answer. For a pragmatist metaphysician, the metaphysical part of the task is pretty much over. The work of pragmatist metaphysics was to provide a characterization of what structure is, such that one could empirically apply that notion to identify structure in the world. The metaphysical tasks did not include specifying which precise patterns one should use. The pragmatist metaphysician could ﬁnd pattern A, then decide to reﬁne the identiﬁcation conditions for pattern A slightly, into pattern A', and ﬁnd instances of pattern A' as well. The metaphysics of 'pattern' just provides the bare schemata of structure. It does not specify which structures one will ﬁnd, nor which to go looking for; those remain empirical questions. Importantly, the metaphysical deﬁnition of structure in terms of patterns does not provide the resources to decide which of A and A' are 'the' correct pattern for the structure, because both have successfully identiﬁed structure. Both are, minimally, real. 5   It is entirely possible for both pattern variations to pick out genuine structure in the world. A realist pragmatist patterns metaphysics In this section, I will argue that there is a better way to be minimal about metaphysics by allowing in all sorts of stuﬀ as legitimate structure, and then doing the hard work of narrowing it further using methodological and empirical inquiry. Pragmatist patterns metaphysics follows in the footsteps of Dennett's pattern ir/realism, where there is very little to which we commit when we say some structure is real. In the face of possible density of structure, a pragmatist metaphysician leans heavily on further considerations to direct inquiry: which patterns are worth the time to deﬁne, or look for, and meet other desiderata for modelling or prediction or discovery? Pragmatism makes a place for our own interests in deciding what to investigate, not because those interests 'make' the patterns real, but because there is so much that is real and comparatively so little time that we should take all the additional constraints we can ﬁnd to further narrow the space of possible avenues of inquiry.  The question that should be asked here is not which of some cluster around pattern A of A, A', A*, and so on, is 'the' metaphysically right pattern. The pragmatist can accept that overlapping identiﬁable structure, because counting as structure doesn't signify much on a weak notion of realism. In general, there will be lots of ﬁndable structure, and most of it is pretty useless. What we want, in the sciences especially, is something like genuine structure that also generalizes eﬀectively, or has a lower noise tolerance, or has a higher noise tolerance but is much easier to check for. Having several viable candidates for pattern variations is a great improvement over insisting that there can be only one. This is where the sciences then iteratively reﬁne the pattern identiﬁcation conditions among potential variants, better ﬁtting patterns together into cohesive ontologies, and ﬁtting those ontologies onto the world. An empirical example is helpful. Consider Chua's (2021) discussion of slight variations in identiﬁcation conditions for entropy in thermodynamics and statistical mechanics. Each of these deﬁnitions essentially oﬀer a slight variation of a pattern, akin to pattern A, A', A*. Each gets at something important, and is well-conﬁrmed: each identiﬁes real structure. Mostly, physicists can use them without having to be concerned about the ways in which they diﬀer; some deﬁnitions are better suited to one physical regime than another. But that does not settle one as the 'right' deﬁnition of entropy. Part of what entropy does is connect a series of other patterns in a rich tapestry that involves conceptual connections (such as equations in which the pattern is related to others) as well as experimental practices for measuring in various conditions or regimes. It sits at a nexus of other patterns as well as actions, interests, and other considerations of scientists. It is the way in which these deﬁnitions could fulﬁll these various functions at that particular nexus that answers questions about which version best suits a particular context of use. There isn't a further metaphysical case that one is uniquely correct; they are all correct, for something, and the process of reﬁning the

A similar point can be found in the context of quantum wavefunctions and real ontology as a response by Mulder (2024) to Wallace (2012). Mulder points to the way in which Wallace ﬁxes the metaphysical criteria, via what Wallace calls Dennett's Criterion, in order to reduce the amount of structure that thereby results from adoptingDennettian patterns. Mulder's raises the concern that this unnecessarily restricts patterns that otherwise meet the criterion. This is a further example of how the presupposition of Sparsity, in a patterns context with a comparatively generous upper limit on how much structure there could be, can yet be worked into the metaphysical deﬁnition of what structure is, so as to not exceed some a priori upper limit.  Chang (e.g. 2004, 2022) also provides a realist pragmatist perspective that similarly defuses the concern that being able to deﬁne more patterns, or, more concepts, somehow means we thereby will them into existence. He repeatedly demonstrates how our cognitive limitations, goals, interests, and historical situatedness play ineliminable roles in the content and structure of scientiﬁc products like models, theories, etc., without thereby undermining the epistemic legitimacy of those products. Our languages, including formal languages and mathematics, are mind-framed. This does not, though, mean that these are thereby mind-determined. And, there are no non-mind-framed options to use. We can make up whatever patterns we like, but that does not mean we will actually ﬁnd instances of them in the world, nor does it mean that we can simply will them into being useful, into making good contributions to science, or being applicable outside some incredibly narrow and gerrymandered range of application. The sciences aim to ﬁt mind-framed representations onto the world, which greatly constrains what makes it out of the metaphysical starting gates. Pragmatism emphasizes methodological considerations such as holism to provide constraints on which structures to investigate. These inquiry- and inquirer-speciﬁc considerations must be involved when there is so much potentially ﬁndable structure: we only have so much in terms of cognitive resources, time, and so on. We have to decide what we want to focus on. There are frequent misunderstandings of pragmatism as thereby putting human-concerns ahead of more 'objective' considerations. There is a suspicion that being a pragmatist and emphasizing the role we play in choosing what patterns to use, reﬁne them, and investigate, compromises the resulting science. These human-framed considerations are sometimes dismissed as merely pragmatic. This is a misunderstanding. Being a pragmatist is not the same as being pragmatic; the pragmatist has eﬀective metaphysical criteria for ﬁnding structure. Pragmatist metaphysics doesn't oﬀer methodological and conceptual considerations in lieu of ﬁnding genuine structure. It oﬀers these considerations in addition to ﬁnding genuine structure, because they are needed to further focus our epistemic eﬀorts. The task of ﬁnding genuine structure still leaves us with far too much that we could ﬁnd, a potentially inexhaustible richness, most of which is not especially helpful in science. We have to invoke other considerations in order to proceed, and are better oﬀ being upfront and explicit about this, because it improves how we do it.

It is simply false to think that The Right (Sparse) Structure is 'truly' objective, so that involving considerations about our own interests and goals involves compromising objectivity with human subjectivity. We already have to make these choices. It is a false dichotomy to claim that either we get at real structure, or we compromise, in a merely pragmatic (not pragmatist) way. We can acknowledge that our limitations and interests play an ineliminable role in our investigations of structure. A fully pragmatist metaphysics is both/and: the sciences can get at real structure with patterns, for example, and our interests and limitations still must play an ineliminable role because not all real structure is equally useful and it is impossible to exhaust all the structure that could be found.  Disentangling these questions about structure acknowledges this interconnection and streamlines the part involving metaphysics, so that it does not attempt to provide a priori answers to questions better left to the sciences. Patterns provide one way of answering this question, surprisingly similar to a point made by David Lewis and realism about possible worlds. Lewis' reasoning was that one only needed to be a realist about one kind of thing, namely, worlds. There were a lot of them, but still one kind of thing. The ontology list of "what there is", involving all the worlds, was long, but the criterion used to decide what got on that list was very minimal. Pattern metaphysics is similarly streamlined. There are many diﬀerent kinds of patterns that can be deﬁned, and many instances in the world of well-deﬁned patterns, such that we might have a lot of diﬀerent ontology lists and they might get quite full. But the metaphysics is minimal: the criteria to get on an ontology list is 'pattern', with identiﬁcation and noise tolerance speciﬁed; the rest is empirical. The pragmatist metaphysician then diﬀers from a post-Lewisian analytic metaphysician. The latter might attempt to develop additional criteria, such as fundamentality or naturalness, in order to get further on the question of genuine versus derivative structure. This metaphysical move fuses two questions, using modiﬁcations to metaphysical criteria for identifying genuine structure to get into an already-determined correct range of sparsity regarding how much structure there is. The more empirically inclined pragmatist would instead provide a metaphysical answer to the issue of how to discern genuine structure, and then use that to inform explicitly methodological considerations, where the metaphysical would constrain, but only partially, investigation into what structure there. But, importantly, it would not constrain or guide with respect to how much we expect to ultimately ﬁnd, such that ﬁnding more is somehow a problematic outcome that should be 'corrected' by changing the metaphysical criteria to result in less structure. From the other direction, the naturalized metaphysics approach (e.g. rainforest realism) holds that we should read oﬀ our metaphysics from our best current scientiﬁc theories. Like the post-Lewisian commitment to sparsity and uniqueness, though, this is not minimal in terms of metaphysics. This naturalized metaphysics procedure might get us the fewest items on a list of the furniture of the world. Yet it requires more substantial commitments regarding the metaphysical principle(s) or criteria by which something makes it onto that list, since those diﬀer between diﬀerent models or

Conclusion: Pluralism, Realism, and Pragmatist Metaphysics Contemporary versions of pragmatism for philosophy of science (inter alia, Andersen and Mitchell 2023, Chang 2022) emphasize the inextricable interconnections between metaphysical and epistemological or methodological considerations. Just as we can't learn from epistemologically isolated metaphysics about how much structure, or which structures, there are in the world, we cannot do pure methodology that involves no metaphysical commitments. If we try, we still end up doing metaphysics, just implicitly and poorly. We can utilize that interconnectivity to improve how we do both by how they mutually constrain one another.  There is a prima facie tension within pragmatism between pluralism and realism. Pluralism with respect to diﬀerent models, theories, ontologies, etc., means allowing for the ongoing coexistence of representations that conﬂict to some degree. Especially when incompatible models involve overlapping domains of phenomena, there will be competing claims about what parts of the world are like that cannot be neatly reconciled. One realist move is to commit to all but one being ultimately eliminable, reducible, or otherwise inferior, thereby resolving the tension. The embrace of pluralism, to such a realist, seems too close to endorsing contradictions or to giving up on resolution. When it allows for the ongoing existence of partially conﬂicting models, pluralism seems to reject the very possibility of there being one world behind those representations.   However, the pluralist can also be realist to a modest degree now, rather than having to wait to ﬁgure out which is the 'right' theory or model. Patterns pragmatism shows how apparently conﬂicting ontologies can all have found some real structure, without having to decide which one is more 'right', and while still maintaining the underlying unity of the world, because that world is rich enough with genuine structure that these descriptions are all getting at parts of it but will never, even collectively, exhaust it).
