Biological Object as Real Patterns: Reconciling Processualism and
Scientific Realism[1]

Yihan Jiang,

Institute of Philosophy, Chinese Academy of sciences (CASIP)

Abstract

In recent years, the philosophy of biology has undergone a significant
shift known as the ‘processual turn,’ largely influenced by the works of
John Dupré and his collaborators (Dupré, 2013, 2020; Nicholson and
Dupré, 2018; Nicholson, 2019). Processualism argues that dynamic
processes, rather than static objects, form the fundamental ontology of
biological reality. This perspective, however, appears to be in tension
with scientific realism, at least in its standard form, which is
committed to the existence of discrete, mind-independent entities as
posited in scientific theories. To reconcile these perspectives, I
propose that effective realism, particularly the framework based on the
real pattern account (Ladyman and Ross 2007; Wallace 2010), offers a
promising solution. According to this account, real patterns are defined
by their utility—they are patterns that are indispensable for
formulating useful generalizations, enabling us to explain and predict
phenomena efficiently. These patterns are objective features of the
world, but their recognition depends on the stance or perspective we
adopt, which is shaped by our explanatory purposes and cognitive tools.
By understanding biological objects as patterns of processes—stable,
emergent features within the dynamic flux of underlying processes—we can
maintain a (minimal) realist commitment to the entities posited by
scientific theories without abandoning the processualist ontology.
Drawing on the real pattern account, I argue that interpreting objects
as patterns of processes resolves the tension between processualism and
scientific realism, affirming the reality of biological objects while
preserving the primacy of processes. Meanwhile, this paper extends the
application of the ​real patterns framework​ by demonstrating its utility
in addressing specific challenges in the philosophy of biology. In doing
so, it highlights how this Dennettian legacy continues to profoundly
enhance our understanding of the fundamental reality revealed by the
sciences.

Key Words

Processualism; Scientific Realism; Real Patterns; Philosophy of Biology;
Metaphysics

Introduction

In recent years, the philosophy of biology has undergone a significant
shift known as the ‘processual turn,’ largely influenced by the works of
John Dupré and his collaborators (Dupré, 2013, 2020; Nicholson and
Dupré, 2018; Nicholson, 2019). This emerging perspective challenges the
traditional ontological framework that prioritizes discrete entities
with intrinsic properties (e.g., Psillos 1999; Chakravartty 2007;
2017a)[2]. Instead, it posits that the ontology of biology should be
grounded in dynamic processes that unfold over temporal durations. This
processual approach emphasizes the fluid, interactive, and dynamic
nature of biological phenomena.

The processual turn has garnered considerable support among philosophers
of biology and biologists alike, who recognize its potential to address
longstanding conceptual limitations in the field. By shifting the focus
from static entities to dynamic processes, this framework provides a
more accurate representation of biological reality, which is inherently
characterized by change, interdependence, and temporal development. As a
result, the processual approach is increasingly regarded as a
paradigmatic framework within the philosophy of biology, with the
potential to reshape how biological phenomena are conceptualized and
studied.

Despite its growing influence, the revolutionary nature of processualism
necessitates significant philosophical work to establish its
compatibility with widely accepted views in the philosophy of science.
One of the most pressing challenges in this regard is its apparent
tension with scientific realism. This tension arises because biological
theories are predominantly framed in object-oriented terms, positing
entities such as DNA, cells, and viruses. Scientific realism, in its
standard form, asserts that our scientific theories (approximately)
describe the mind-independent, objective world as it truly is. This view
takes scientific statements at ‘face value,’ maintaining that the
entities posited by scientific theories refer to real, mind-independent
objects ‘out there’ in the world. The metaphysical picture endorsed by
scientific realists is typically object-oriented. For example, Psillos
(1999, 2001) argues that ‘the world we live in (and science cares about)
is made of individuals, properties, and their relations’ (2001, S23; see
also Franklin and Robertson 2024)[3]. The primary rationale for
scientific realism stems from the need to explain the success of
science. It is argued that the ability of scientific theories to make
accurate novel predictions and guide technological advancements requires
an explanation, and the best explanation is that these theories are true
descriptions of the world (Putnam 1975; Chakravartty 2017b).

Given the object-oriented ontology that underpins scientific realism,
which appears to conflict with processualism’s emphasis on dynamic
processes, a critical question arises: Can processualism reconcile its
commitments with scientific realism? Specifically, can it maintain
realism about scientific theories and their posited entities, thereby
explaining the success of science? Without a satisfactory answer,
processualism risks being perceived as less appealing or even
incompatible with the empirical and explanatory successes of scientific
practice. One might argue that a realist stance toward biological
objects presupposes an ontology in which objects exist independently and
primitively, possessing intrinsic properties—a view seemingly at odds
with processualism’s dynamic framework. This apparent incompatibility
could lead to the conclusion that processualism cannot align with
scientific realism.

However, I argue that this dichotomy is false. It is not necessarily the
case that realism about objects requires an ontologically ‘thick’
interpretation, where objects are understood as independent, primitive
entities with intrinsic properties. Instead, the reality of objects can
be understood in functionalist terms. Drawing on Dennett’s real patterns
account (1991), many philosophers have argued that objects can be
conceptualized as patterns, and the reality of such patterns depends on
their usefulness in explaining and predicting phenomena (e.g., Wallace
2010). Building on this insight, I propose that processualism can adopt
a scientific realist stance by reinterpreting objects as stable, real
patterns emerging from underlying dynamic processes. This approach
allows processualism to affirm the reality of scientific entities
without committing to an object-oriented ontology, thereby reconciling
its processual framework with the explanatory success of science. By
understanding objects as patterns of processes, processualism can
maintain its commitment to dynamism while accommodating the realist
intuition that scientific theories accurately describe a
mind-independent reality.

This synthesis offers three significant advantages. ​First, it resolves
the tension between processualism and scientific realism, strengthening
processualism’s position as a viable framework in the philosophy of
biology. By providing a processualist account that aligns with
scientific realism, it demonstrates how processualism can engage more
effectively with the ontological commitments of contemporary science.
​Second, it offers a valuable contribution to those committed to
scientific realism by presenting a view that better aligns with the
philosophy of biology. Although processual ontology has increasingly
become the dominant paradigm in the field, scientific realists have yet
to engage seriously with its implications. This paper represents a first
attempt to bridge this gap, providing a realist account that is
compatible with the processual turn in contemporary biology. Third, it
extends the application of the ​real patterns framework​ by demonstrating
its utility in addressing specific ontological and epistemic challenges
in the philosophy of biology. By showcasing how this concept can solve
problems in this domain, I highlight its broader potential as a robust
theoretical tool. Together, these benefits underscore the value of
understanding objects as real patterns of processes, offering a nuanced
and integrative perspective that advances both processualism and
scientific realism.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, I introduce
processualism and its core ontological commitments. In Section 3, I
discuss how the object-oriented ontology derived from scientific realism
poses a challenge to processualism. Section 4 reviews existing attempts
by processualists to secure realism within their framework, along with
my critique that more needs to be said to differentiate their stance
from anti-realist positions, such as instrumentalism. In Section 5, I
argue that effective realism offers a potential solution to this
problem. Section 6 introduces a specific form of effective realism based
on the real pattern account. In the final two sections, I present my
view on how interpreting objects as real patterns of processes can help
processualism maintain a realist commitment to them.

What is Processualism

Contemporary biological processualism has emerged and gained significant
influence in recent years, building on the process ontology of the 20th
century, primarily developed by Nicholas Rescher, Johanna Seibt, Dan
Nicholson, and John Dupré, along with their collaborators (Rescher,
1996; Seibt, 2009; Dupré, 2013, 2020; Nicholson and Dupré, 2018;
Nicholson 2019). Processualism advocates an ontological shift from
objects to processes, claiming that all that exists are processes.
Processualism thinks that everything is in flux. It believes dynamicity
is more fundamental and stability is derived from dynamicity (see Seibt,
2023). According to processualism, processes are dynamic and extended in
time. They conform to some philosophers’ characterization of
“occurrents”, a kind of existences that have different temporal parts
strung together over time[4] (Lewis, 2001).

Numerous arguments have been advanced in support of processualism, with
one of the key motivations stemming from the observation that living
systems are inherently interdependent rather than independent. Dupré and
Nicholson (2018) argue that two features typically attributed to
objects—having clear boundaries and exhibiting a degree of autonomy or
independence (p. 20)—are largely inapplicable in the biological realm.
These features are challenged by the fact that biological systems are
not isolated or self-sufficient entities; rather, they exist in a state
of deep interdependence. This interdependence is exemplified by the
prevalence of symbiotic relationships. For instance, the normal
functioning of many systems within the human body relies on cooperative
interactions with symbiotic microbes. This raises significant questions
about where the boundaries of human organisms should be drawn, as the
inclusion of these microbes is, to a considerable extent, indeterminate.

A more detailed illustration of this point is provided by Dupré and
Guttinger (2016), who specifically discuss the case of viruses. They
propose that viruses should be regarded as processes rather than stable
entities with intrinsic properties. When we think of viruses, we
typically envision virions, i.e., the stable, inert state in which
viruses exist when not actively interacting with host cells. A virion
consists of a core of nucleic acid (RNA or DNA) surrounded by a protein
coat, or capsid, which protects the viral genetic material and
facilitates attachment to host cells. However, the virion state
represents only one phase of the virus’s lifecycle. Upon attaching to a
host, the virus undergoes a dramatic transformation, initiating a series
of events that often lead to the destruction of the host cell and the
production of numerous new virions. In some cases, the viral DNA
integrates into the host genome, replicating alongside it during cell
division. This integration blurs the distinction between the virus and
the host, raising questions about whether the viral genome should be
considered an independent entity or a component of the host genome.
Given this fluidity and the difficulty of drawing clear boundaries,
Dupré and Guttinger argue that viruses are better understood as
processes rather than discrete objects. They state, “rather than worry
whether an episome, or a piece of naked DNA replicating in its host
cell, is a virus, we should simply recognize that it is part of the
viral process. It is because of this belonging to a specific process
that it qualifies as ‘viral’ DNA” (p. 113).

Moreover, processualists argue that the principle of interdependence
applies to all levels of biological organization, including entities
typically regarded as objects, such as organs. For instance, the heart,
often seen as a discrete biological entity, is better understood as a
dynamic process according to processualists, because its normal
functioning depends on its integration within broader systems, such as
the respiratory and circulatory systems, highlighting its reliance on
continuous interactions rather than isolated existence. Consequently,
processual ontologists reject the notion of biological objects as
entities with intrinsic properties altogether, arguing all that exist
are dynamic processes. (Dupré, 2011, 2016, 2018, 2020, 2021b)

Despite its compelling motivations, the ontological framework of
processualism generates a significant tension with the ontological
commitments held by many scientific realists. In the following section,
I will elaborate on this tension and demonstrate how it poses a
challenge to processualism.

Scientific Realism and Metaphysics

A significant challenge faced by processualism arises from the
object-centered nature of biological practice. In biological research,
scientists routinely employ object-terms, such as molecules, cells, and
organisms, and the advancement of biological understanding is often
driven by the discovery and characterization of mechanisms involving
discrete entities (Machamer, Darden, and Craver 2000)[5]. The practical
and explanatory success of such object-centered methodologies appears to
lend strong support to a metaphysics that privileges stable,
identifiable objects. For the processualist, then, a pressing question
is how to account for the remarkable success of scientific practices
that are deeply committed to object-based representations.

For many philosophers of science, the success of science is best
explained through a realist lens. This intuition is most famously
encapsulated in the “no miracles” argument, as formulated by Hilary
Putnam: “The positive argument for realism is that it is the only
philosophy that doesn’t make the success of science a miracle” (Putnam
1975, p. 73). The argument relies on inference to the best explanation
(IBE): the empirical success of science—its capacity to generate novel
predictions, produce effective technologies, and systematically explain
natural phenomena—would be miraculous unless its theories are at least
approximately true descriptions of a mind-independent reality.

The standard formulation of scientific realism maintains that scientific
theories aim to (and often do) provide approximately true descriptions
of an objective, mind-independent world. On this view, theoretical terms
are interpreted literally, and the entities they refer to are understood
as real, independent objects. Psillos (1999) has defended a
causal-descriptive theory of reference to account for how scientific
terms latch onto real entities. According to this view, reference is
secured by a “core causal description” that captures the essential
properties of an entity in virtue of its causal role in generating
observable phenomena (p. 295). This account assumes a metaphysics of
individuals with intrinsic properties, exemplified by Psillos’s
treatment of electrons as discrete objects with well-defined attributes
such as mass and charge. As he puts it, “the world we live in (and
science cares about) is made of individuals, properties, and their
relations” (Psillos 2001, S23; see also 1999).

This metaphysical framework is further supported by Chakravartty (2007,
2017), who argues that realism requires commitment to mind-independent
entities whose causal powers ground the success of scientific theories.
In his semirealist position, objects function as the “nodes” within
causal structures, bearing dispositional properties that underwrite
empirical regularities. For Chakravartty, realism is not merely a
semantic thesis about truth but an ontological commitment to the
existence of entities that possess causal powers and are detectable via
scientific instrumentation.

In addition, the rapid rise of the new mechanistic philosophy, as a
promising metaphysical framework for biology, has also reinforced this
object-oriented outlook. Many proponents of New Mechanism (e.g., Glennan
2017) argue that the explanatory power of mechanisms lies in their
accurate representation of real entities and their interactions.
Mechanisms are successful, on this view, precisely because the
components they invoke correspond to actual entities in the world. These
philosophers therefore uphold a version of scientific realism that
affirms the reality of the objects postulated by science.

Viruses are among the entities frequently discussed in debates about
scientific realism. Very recently, Franklin and Robertson (2024) stated
at the beginning of their paper that, “If anything is real, viruses are
real. That’s because viruses enter into robust generalizations that are
outstandingly empirically successful. And it is just this empirical
success that motivates the scientific realist’s commitment to them” (pp.
60–61). They focus on a specific virus, the Tobacco Mosaic Virus (TMV),
which is well known for its ability to damage tobacco crops. According
to Klug (1999), TMV has a rod-like structure that “results from its
basic design, namely a regular helical array of identical protein
subunits, within which is embedded a single molecule of RNA wound as a
helix” (p. 531). Furthermore, Liu and Nelson (2013) explain that
“Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) encodes four known functional proteins: the
126 and 183 kDa replication-associated proteins, the movement protein
(MP), and the structural capsid or coat protein (CP).” Scientists have
also identified the mechanisms by which TMV causes disease in tobacco
crops (Liu and Nelson 2013). For scientific realists, the fact that
TMV’s causal relationships with specific diseases are empirically
testable, and that TMV can be used as a tool to manipulate and
investigate biological processes, provides strong grounds for adopting a
realist stance toward its existence[6]. The empirical success of
theories involving TMV, they argue, would be inexplicable if TMV were
not a real, mind-independent, and causally efficacious entity.

Such realist attitudes, which are committed to an object-oriented
metaphysics, stand in fundamental contrast to processualism, which holds
that dynamic processes, rather than static objects, constitute the
fundamental structure of the world. This contrast gives rise to a
pressing question: can processualism nonetheless sustain a realist
attitude toward biological objects, in order to account for the success
of biological theories? As discussed earlier, processualists argue that
the ontology underlying biological phenomena involving viruses is better
understood in terms of dynamic processes, but can it also maintain a
realist stance toward viruses—a kind of entities that scientists have
identified and studied extensively? In the next section, I will review
how the leading processualist, John Dupré, has approached this issue,
along with my critique of his position.

Realism in a Processual Ontology

Dupré supports a form of realism he calls “cautious realism” or
“qualified realism” (2016). Yet, as I will argue, his position remains
tenuous and under-specified in crucial respects. Consider Dupré’s
discussion of DNA. He observes that the familiar three-dimensional
structure of DNA, often used to teach students about its structure and
function, is far from a complete representation of reality. In reality,
the genome comprises not only the helix itself but also histones (the
proteins forming the core of chromosomes) and a host of other
dynamically associated molecules that critically influence genomic
behavior. Moreover, the genome is not a static entity but highly
dynamic, with its shape and behavior constantly changing. Thus, the
conventional model represents merely a snapshot of one transient state
within an ever-changing system. Nevertheless, Dupré maintains that this
simplified representation manifests partial truth—it does correspond to
aspects of the real biological phenomenon, even though it omits much.
From this he infers that we ought to adopt a realist stance toward the
DNA model, but this realism needs to be more cautious than the standard
scientific realism, acknowledging the limitations and partiality of such
representations.

However, Dupré’s quick and somewhat cursory endorsement of this
“cautious realism” raises several significant problems. First, it is
unclear how this position fits within the broader scientific realism
debate and how it relates to other existing views, such as standard
scientific realism. Resolving this question is not straightforward.
Second, and more problematically, can one coherently deny that objects
are merely temporary stabilizations of processes (see also Dupré and
O’Malley, 2007)—essentially claiming they do not fundamentally
exist—while simultaneously asserting that these objects represent
aspects of reality? This tension raises the worry that objects, in this
view, might simply be epistemic tools or instruments created to help
explain certain phenomena, rather than representations of
mind-independent reality. If so, Dupré’s “cautious realism” risks
collapsing into instrumentalism (Rowbottom, 2017), a position that
denies the reality of theoretical entities and treats them merely as
useful constructs for prediction and explanation. This ambiguity calls
for a more rigorous defense of how processualism can reconcile its
dynamic ontology with a realist commitment to the partial reality of
biological objects[7].

The problem of how Dupré’s realism differs from instrumentalism becomes
more pressing when we consider another key feature of his view: his
commitment to pluralism. Dupré argues that there is no single, objective
way to carve nature into individual biological entities. Instead, there
are multiple, equally justified ways to do so, and the resulting
entities are all equally real. This idea is closely tied to his concept
of promiscuous individualism. As Dupré illustrates in an example from
his work:

“Consider a lichen. This consists of a fungus (a multicellular organism)
and a very large number of photosynthetic bacteria. Presumably all of
these—the fungus, all of the bacteria, and the lichen itself—are
individuals. What about the population of bacteria? Or subpopulations
with a particular mutation? Or what about the lichen-covered tree of
which this particular lichen is part? There surely could be reasons for
distinguishing any of these individuals, and ways in which the entities
composing them could be integrated and differentiated from other
entities that could ground such distinctions” (2021b, p. 44).

In this example, Dupré suggests that there are multiple valid ways to
identify different entities within a lichen, depending on the context
and the questions being asked. Importantly, he emphasizes that the act
of carving up nature into distinct entities has a “subjective,
anthropocentric aspect.” In other words, how we divide nature depends on
human purposes, interests, and the specific phenomena we aim to explain.
This pragmatic element is central to Dupré’s pluralism, as it allows for
diverse and context-dependent ways of understanding biological reality.
According to Dupré, the entities resulting from these different ways of
carving up nature are all equally real, a position that aligns with his
processual ontology and his rejection of object-based metaphysics.

However, this pluralistic and pragmatic approach raises a significant
challenge: how does Dupré’s realism about objects differ from
instrumentalism? Dupré bears the burden of clarifying why the entities
identified through these various “carvings” are not simply human-created
devices for explaining phenomena but instead correspond to something
real in the world. Without further elaboration, his position risks being
indistinguishable from instrumentalism, which denies the objective
reality of theoretical entities and views them solely as epistemic tools
or constructs for prediction and explanation.

In my view, to address this challenge, processualists must provide a
more detailed account of how objects, despite being context-dependent
and pragmatically identified, nevertheless capture objective features of
reality. While Dupré asserts that objects “partially represent reality,”
this claim remains underdeveloped. Without specifying which objective
features these representations correspond to, his cautious realism risks
amounting to little more than paying lip service to scientific realism,
offering no substantive advancement or resolution to the debate. While I
am sympathetic to the idea that processualism is compatible with
scientific realism, I believe that its supporters must articulate how
this pluralistic and processual framework can uphold a robust realism
about biological objects while avoiding the pitfalls of instrumentalism.
In the rest of the paper, I aim to provide such a development, offering
a clearer and more rigorous account of how processualism can reconcile
its dynamic ontology with a realist commitment to biological objects.

Effective Realism

To summarize, processualism faces a two-horned dilemma in sustaining a
realist attitude toward scientific theories. On the one hand, standard
scientific realism, anchored in an object-oriented metaphysics, stands
in fundamental opposition to a processual ontology and thus cannot be
embraced by processualists. On the other hand, the alternative realist
positions advanced by processualists (such as Dupré’s) remain opaque
regarding their commitment to realism and risk collapsing into
instrumentalism.

I argue that a more promising route to reconciling processualism with a
realist interpretation of biological objects lies in functionalism or,
more recently, what has been termed effective realism. Effective realism
has gained widespread support, particularly among phyiscs-informed
philosophers of science (Ladyman and Ross 2007; Wallace 2010,
forthcoming; Williams 2019; Fraser 2018, 2020; Eggs 2019; Saatsi 2021;
Robertson and Wilson 2024; Ladyman and Lorenzetti 2024), as a way to
make sense of non-fundamental ontology and clarify its relationship with
fundamental ontology.

In Dupré and Nicholson (2018), an illuminating analogy is drawn between
the relationship of substance ontology to processual ontology and that
of classical physics to modern physics:

“Beyond any such inherent bias, we surmise that the widespread
prevalence of substance ontology also reflects the fact that in many
circumstances it does the job sufficiently well. The relation between
substance and process ontology is not completely unlike the relation
between classical and modern physics. Just as classical physics provides
a convenient approximation of middle-sized physical entities moving at
relatively slow speeds but does not constitute an accurate description
of physical reality, so substance ontology provides a serviceable
characterization of biological entities, especially when considered over
short temporal intervals, despite being a fundamentally inappropriate
description of the living world” (p. 39).

This analogy points to a persistent challenge in the philosophy of
science: How should we understand and remain realist about the
theoretical entities posited by once-successful yet ultimately
superseded theories? Throughout the history of science, numerous
theories (including classical physics) have been superseded by more
accurate or comprehensive successors. Yet most realists agree that the
entities posited by these superseded theories, much like how
processualists view biological objects, at least partially describe the
objective world correctly. The central question, then, is this: If we
are to remain realists about the entities of an approximately true
theory, what must the nature of that realism be?

In response to this question, Wilson and Robertson (2024) propose a form
of effective realism about ‘theoretical relicts’, the term they used to
refer to entities posited by superseded theories. They argue that past
theories that remain explanatorily useful or non-redundant should be
understood as effective theories, which “aspire to describe phenomena
only within a limited range and to a limited range of accuracy” (p. 17).
Effective theories, in contrast to more fundamental theories, describe
the same target phenomena in a more abstract, coarse-grained manner.
Consequently, the theoretical relics of these theories are understood to
effectively represent some aspects of reality, even if they do not
capture the full or fundamental truth.

One example of such a theoretical relic discussed in their work is space
(as opposed to spacetime). Since the advent of special relativity and
its spacetime interpretation, ordinary three-dimensional space is no
longer regarded as a fundamental component of physical reality. However,
spatial distances and temporal durations are not dismissed as mere
illusions; they remain relevant and continue to play important roles in
explanations within certain higher-level sciences. Newtonian mechanics,
along with its theoretical relics of space and time, is thus validated
as providing accurate descriptions of low-velocity motion, rather than
being purely fictional.

Given that processualists also view object descriptions as abstract,
coarse-grained, and accurate only within certain spatial and temporal
limits, I argue that effective realism offers a promising framework for
understanding objects within a processualist ontology. On this view,
objects are effectively real—they emerge from the underlying dynamic
processes and are indispensable for explaining and predicting phenomena
within specific domains. The specific theories of effective realism I
wish to invoke here are those developed based on Daniel Dennett’s real
patterns account (e.g., Ladyman and Ross 2007; Ladyman and Lorenzetti
2024; Wallace 2010), which has been highly influential and has received
extensive scholarly discussion[8]. I will introduce this account in the
next section.

Real Pattern

Philosophers’ interest in ‘real patterns’ starts with Daniel Dennett’s
1991 paper, where he illustrates the idea of real pattern through the
‘Game of Life,’ designed by mathematician John Conway[9]. The game is
set in a pixelated computer world where the primary rule is that each
pixel can either be on or off. The state of each pixel over time is
determined by an algorithm based on a simple set of rules that consider
the states of neighboring pixels. At the most basic level, all pixels
are controlled deterministically by equations that describe how their
states evolve from initial conditions. In this pixel-level description,
there are discernable but simple patterns defined by the behavior of
individual pixels switching on and off. The bitmap represents the entire
state of the world by showing the exact state of each pixel. However,
when we look beyond the pixel level, there are stable and recognizable
patterns that exist within Conway’s Game of Life. These patterns can be
described with a new vocabulary that isn’t limited to individual pixels.
For example, ‘gliders’ are a type of pattern: they are stable pixel
configurations that go through a cycle of state changes, causing the
glider to “move” across the screen and start the cycle again. Another
type of pattern is “eaters”, which destroy the stability of other
patterns like gliders when they encounter them, thus “eating” the glider
(see Andersen 2017 for a nice summary).

Dennett’s insight is that there is a class of objective,
observer-independent facts about those higher-level objects such as
gliders and eaters such that they are not merely epistemic but really
exist. Although Game of Life operates as a fully deterministic system
with simple rules, calculating the future states of the system based on
these rules and the bitmap can be very costly. However, keeping track of
the system in terms of the higher-level patterns such as gliders and
eaters allows us to make prediction much more efficiently, and it is an
objective fact that a description of the system in terms of higher-level
patterns compresses the information. Thus, Dennett defines a ‘real’
pattern in the following way: “a pattern exists in some data—is real—if
there is a description of the data that is more efficient than the bit
map, whether or not anyone can concoct it” (1991, p. 34). Haugeland
(1998) understands these patterns as referring to an “orderly or
non-random arrangement—the opposite of chaos” (Haugeland 1998, p. 274),
and the order of these arrangements exists objectively and
observer-independently. In other words, the existence of such patterns
in a given patch of pixels is an objective fact.

Critics have pointed out that Dennett’s account of real patterns is
ambiguous, as it remains unclear whether leads to realism or mere
instrumentalism. Subsequent developments have aimed to strengthen the
realist interpretation of patterns. Millhouse (2021), for example,
argues that for a pattern to be considered real, it must not only
provide a more efficient description of a system but also exhibit a
sufficiently high degree of similarity to the system’s physical
structure. In his paper, he further proposes a way to quantify this
similarity.

Despite this, a significant concern remains inherent in Dennett’s
original framework: it implies that any pattern offering a more
compressed description than a raw bitmap qualifies as real. This
permissiveness risks generating an overabundance of ‘real’ patterns,
which dilutes the concept’s realist credentials. Therefore, Laydman and
Ross (2007) contend that while Dennett provides a necessary condition
for real-patternhood—namely, increased descriptive efficiency—he fails
to specify sufficient conditions that would secure a genuinely realist
account. In response, they propose non-redundancy as a key sufficient
condition, attempting to define it via the notion of logical depth. Suñé
and Martínez (2019) later advanced an alternative by defining real
patterns directly in terms of their contribution to reducing the
conditional Kolmogorov complexity of a dataset, offering a potentially
simpler and more effective criterion for non-redundancy.

The debate over how best to define real patterns from the standpoint of
information theory is highly technical and, while important, lies beyond
the scope of this paper. For present purposes, I will take the real
pattern account as an established and sufficiently developed framework
ready for application. My proposal in the subsequent section builds upon
and is ​​compatible with multiple existing, well-developed definitions of
real pattern​​. Readers who subscribe to a particular view of real pattern
are free to adopt whichever definition they find most compelling and
interpret my arguments through that lens. What matters more for the aim
of this paper, however, is the metaphysical interpretation of real
pattern.

Among proponents of real patterns, there is broad agreement that their
reality is grounded not in their material constitution, but in their
usefulness, function, and participation in higher-order regularities.
Haugeland (1998), in his critical engagement with Dennett, emphasizes
that the ontological import of patterns lies in what he calls their
“specialness from above.” This phrase captures the idea that a pattern’s
ontological status as is not determined by its constituent elements but
by how it participates in or contributes to overarching, higher-order
structures and rule-governed systems.

Haugeland illustrate this metaphysical point with the example of chess
pieces. He argues that chess pieces are “manifestly not” substantial
things in the metaphysical sense. The very “being” of a chess piece is
constituted by its functional role: how they move, threaten, protect,
and are captured according to the rules of the game. Their identity is
not tied to their material instantiation—whether as plastic figurines or
pixelated images in a virtual environment. Haugeland asserts that chess
pieces are “strictly speaking, not at all real, not even a little bit,”
yet in a pragmatic or functional sense, they are “genuine, full-fledged
entities, one hundred percent.” This view provides a compelling
framework for thinking about entities whose reality is grounded not in
intrinsic essence but in their functionality. It is especially
well-aligned with and useful for a processualist metaphysics.

Haugeland’s idea of grounding the reality of patterns in their
functional utility has been preserved and further developed by later
proponents of the real pattern account, notably Ladyman (2009, with Ross
2007; with Lorenzetti 2024) and Wallace (2010). In applying the real
pattern framework to the metaphysics of sciences, they think real
patterns are those that play an indispensables role in scientific
theories that successfully explain and predict phenomena. In particular,
real patterns are those feature in counterfactual and nomological
generalizations. Counterfactual generalizations express what would occur
under hypothetical conditions (e.g., “If a tiger were to encounter prey,
it would stalk and hunt it”), while nomological generalizations
articulate law-like regularities in nature (e.g., “Tigers hunt prey to
survive”). These generalizations form the core of scientific theorizing
and are essential to the explanatory and predictive ambitions of the
sciences. For Ladyman, a pattern’s status as real hinges precisely on
its ​​indispensability to such generalizations​​, enabling reliable
prediction and explanation of phenomena (Ladyman 2017, p. 157) [10].

Wallace (2010) offers a similar account, asserting that “a macro-object
is a pattern, and the existence of a pattern as a real thing depends on
the usefulness—in particular, the explanatory power and predictive
reliability—of theories which admit that pattern in their ontology” (p.
6) [11]. Wallace emphasizes that the reality of a pattern—and, by
extension, the objects we ordinarily regard as real—is grounded in its
pragmatic utility. He illustrates this point through the example of
describing and predicting a tiger’s hunting behavior. One could attempt
this at the molecular or cellular level, tracing the behavior of a vast
number of biological constituents of the tiger. Alternatively, one could
operate at the level of the whole animal, modeling its behavior in terms
of observable intentions and ecological contexts (e.g., “a hungry tiger
hunts a deer”). Clearly, the latter approach is vastly more efficient
and explanatory. According to Wallace, it is precisely this efficiency
and the resulting explanatory power that justify treating the tiger,
understood as a macro-pattern, as a real and legitimate object of
scientific inquiry. Crucially, the fact that explanations invoking the
tiger are objectively more efficient than those at lower levels of
description indicates that such patterns are not arbitrary human
constructs, but objective features of the world. (see Seifert 2023 for a
recent application of this idea to chemical bonds).

The criterion for a pattern’s reality—its grounding in explanatory power
and pragmatic utility—directly resonates with the principles of
​​effective realism​​ discussed previously. This deep connection leads
Ladyman and Lorenzetti (2024) to explicitly integrate the accounts. They
contend that the reality ascribed to a pattern by this account is
fundamentally an effective reality. Thus, they argue that ​​the real
patterns framework is best understood as a form of effective
realism[12].

Such kind of realist account differ significantly from standard
scientific realism and the scientific ontology it entails. Unlike
traditional realism, which posits that objects exist independently and
discretely, possessing intrinsic identities, real pattern accounts
define what is real or what exists in terms of explanatory utility
within scientific theories. In other words, the reality of an entity is
grounded in its role in enabling successful explanations and
predictions, rather than in its status as an independent, discrete
object with intrinsic properties. This approach avoids an ontologically
‘thick’ reading of objects, making it more compatible with a processual
framework and opening the possibility for alignment between the two. I
argue that processualism can maintain a realist commitment toward
biological objects by adopting the framework of real pattern accounts,
understanding the existence of objects in a functionalist or effective
sense. In the next section, I will discuss this idea in greater detail.

Interpreting Objects as Real Patterns in a Processual Framework

The kind of ontological picture I envision, where processualism is
combined with a real pattern account, can be understood as a two-level
ontology. At the more fundamental level, the ontology is ‘thicker,’
while at the less fundamental level, it is ‘thinner’ but still robust
enough to support realist claims. In my view, processualism informs us
about the more fundamental level, revealing that the basic ontological
constituents of our world are dynamic processes—entities that are
constantly changing and unfolding over spatiotemporal dimensions (rather
than merely spatial ones) [13]. In other words, these processes
constitute the foundational fabric of reality.

Within this processual world, patterns emerge here and there. These
patterns are not static or independent entities but rather patterns of
processes. They help us track regularities that objectively exist in the
world in a more efficient and informative way. These patterns are what
we describe in object terms, such as cells, viruses, tigers, and so on.
Importantly, these patterns are not mere human constructs or illusions;
they are real in the sense that they objectively exist, compress
information, and support counterfactual generalizations. These patterns
are robust, provide novel explanatory value, and are indispensable for
scientific understanding. In this sense, we can affirm that objects (or
the patterns they track) exist and are real. However, it is crucial to
recognize that their existence is ‘thinner’ and more derivative compared
to processes. The reality of objects should be understood only
functionally rather than substantively. That is, they do not constitute
the fundamental material ‘stuff’ of the world in the way processes do.
Their existence is secondary to the primacy of processes.

Consider the example of viruses. Processualists may very well be correct
in arguing that the biological phenomenon of a virus infecting a host
and causing damage is better understood, from an ontological
perspective, as a dynamic process. However, it is often more
efficient—or even indispensable—to describe this process in object
terms, such as ‘viruses,’ ‘host cells,’ and the causal relationships
between them. This does not mean that viruses are discrete objects with
intrinsic properties, as traditional object-oriented ontologies might
suggest. Nor does it mean that viruses are not real at all. Instead,
viruses should be understood as patterns of processes—patterns that are
indispensable for formulating counterfactual generalizations, which in
turn help us explain, predict, and manipulate the corresponding
phenomena. The objective fact that these patterns compress information
and enable us to better explain, predict, and intervene in the world
supports a realist attitude toward them. In other words, while viruses
are not independent, static entities with intrinsic properties, their
reality is grounded in their role as patterns of processes that are
embedded in robust, explanatory frameworks. This perspective allows us
to reconcile the dynamic, processual nature of biological reality with a
realist commitment to the entities posited by scientific theories.

Let’s examine another example in depth to show how biological objects
should be treated as real patterns, compressing the information of
underlying processes, and in doing so dramatically enhance our ability
to predict and manipulate living systems. In contemporary evolutionary
and developmental biology, the classical doctrine of molecular genetics,
which is often dubbed “gene–trait atomism” (Difrisco & Jaeger 2019), is
now recognized as an oversimplification. According to this older view,
DNA is transcribed into mRNA, translated into protein, and thereby gives
rise to discrete phenotypic traits in a straightforward, one-to-one
correspondence between genotype and phenotype (Griffiths and Stotz 2013,
ch. 3; Huang 2012). By contrast, we now appreciate that
genotype-phenotype mapping is neither linear nor deterministic but is
instead embedded within a highly interconnected, nonlinear system of
regulatory interactions.

At the heart of this revised picture lie gene regulatory networks
(GRNs), which model how genes influence one another through promotion,
inhibition, and other regulatory mechanisms (Jaeger & Monk 2014). GRNs
reveal that phenotypic outcomes emerge from the self-organized dynamics
of thousands of genes, each reciprocally affecting and being affected by
its peers (Huang et al. 2005). Moreover, gene expression is not solely a
function of endogenous interactions: it is modulated by non-coding RNAs,
symbiotic microbiota, protein feedback, and extracellular factors such
as nutrition, temperature, and light (Griffiths & Stotz 2013, ch. 5).
Thus, the same genome can yield different phenotypes in different
contexts, underscoring the context-sensitivity of developmental
processes.

A processualist understanding of genes arguably dates back to Waddington
(1957), but it is Griffiths and Stotz (2018) who bring this insight to
the fore in contemporary philosophy of biology. They explicitly defend a
processual ontology of genetics, arguing that genes should be understood
not as discrete, self-contained entities but as dynamic processes
embedded within the ongoing flux of developmental interactions. On their
view, genes are not primary building blocks but are defined by and
intelligible only within the continuous flux of developmental
interactions. Dynamics, they contend, is not an ancillary feature of
developmental systems but their very essence[14].

However, despite the widespread appeal of a processual ontology of
genetics, the day-to-day practice of experimental genetics remains
overwhelmingly gene-centric. Most genetic interventions, such as gene
editing, gene knockouts, transgenic insertions, are justified and
interpreted in terms of discrete DNA segments whose manipulation
produces predictable phenotypic effects. For instance, Cohen and Boyer’s
classic experiments involved transferring two distinct DNA fragments
into Escherichia coli strains that were originally antibiotic-sensitive;
upon acquisition, these cells became resistant, leading Cohen and Boyer
to designate the inserts as “antibiotic resistance genes” on the
strength of their direct causal efficacy (Cohen et al. 1973).

Philosophers have accordingly emphasized the special causal status of
genes. Waters (2007) contends that DNA sequences function as genuine
difference-makers for phenotypic traits, since precise changes in
nucleotide order produce equally precise shifts in RNA outputs (p. 574).
Woodward (2010) builds on this idea, framing it within his
interventionist theory of causation. He emphasizes that the specificity
of DNA’s role lies in the fine-grained control it exerts over protein
synthesis. According to Woodward, the many possible states of a DNA
sequence are systematically associated with different corresponding
states of mRNA molecules. This means that variations in the DNA sequence
enable precise control over which RNA molecules or proteins are
synthesized. Waters further insists that this manipulability reflects an
ontological distinctiveness: genes are “used as levers to manipulate and
investigate a wide variety of biological processes” (Waters 2008, p.
259), supporting an object-based interpretation of genes. Yet such
commitments sit uneasily alongside a fully processual view of
development (See Jiang, forthcoming, for a detailed discussion).

The widespread success of gene-centric mechanisms and their
experimentally verified causal efficacy thus poses a serious challenge
for processualist accounts. On the one hand, the very real predictive
and manipulative power of gene-editing, knockout, and related techniques
renders any wholesale anti-realism about genes untenable. On the other
hand, the standard scientific-realist insistence that genes are
discrete, world-existing objects seems at odds with a processual
ontology in which dynamic interactions—not static entities—are primary.

This apparent tension, however, dissolves once we reconceive genes and
their associated mechanisms as real patterns manifesting within
particular spatiotemporal scales (cf. Ladyman & Ross). From this
perspective, a mechanism involving a discrete DNA segment does not carve
nature at its joints by isolating an immutable object, but rather
identifies a stable, reproducible pattern of regulatory activity
embedded in a broader developmental process. In principle, one could
model the entire, high-dimensional gene-regulatory system—accounting for
feedback loops, environmental inputs (nutrition, temperature, light,
etc.), and intercellular interaction—to predict phenotypic outcomes with
maximal precision. In practice, however, we exploit the fact that
localized regulatory modules reliably emerge: by isolating these
“stable” subnetworks, we uncover counterfactual interdependencies
between specific gene-patterns and traits under defined conditions.
These patterns provide robust levers for intervention, enabling
efficient prediction and manipulation, while remaining fully compatible
with a processual ontology in which systems are best understood as
networks of processes. This hierarchical determination of genetic
identity resonates with Haugeland’s concept of ‘specialness from above,’
much like how chess pieces derive their functional significance not from
their physical composition but from their position in the game’s
governing structure.

Following this line of reasoning, I propose that genes are real in an
effective sense. I argue that effective realism, grounded in the
real-pattern account, delivers exactly the kind of realism that
processualism requires. First and foremost, it is a realist stance.
According to this view, genes (and other object terms in biology)
accurately describe features of the objective world rather than serving
as mere instrumental or epistemic tools. They capture real patterns,
which are orderly arrangements of processes that exist independently of
our descriptions. These patterns are not illusions or human constructs;
they reflect genuine features of the world that enable us to explain,
predict, and intervene in natural phenomena.

At the same time, this realism is effectively constrained: it is valid
only within particular domains and spatiotemporal scales. Thus, while
genes are indispensable for explaining inheritance, development, and
many molecular manipulations, they are not posited as discrete entities
endowed with intrinsic essences, as many standard scientific realists
maintain. Instead, each “gene” designation picks out a pattern of
regulatory activity that holds reliably under specified conditions. This
avoids any metaphysical commitment to thick, object-based entities,
which would contradict a processual ontology. By embracing effective
realism, processualists can retain the explanatory virtues and
interventionist leverage of object-oriented descriptions yet keep their
core claim that reality consists fundamentally of dynamic processes.

Accommodating Pluralism

Furthermore, another advantage of the real pattern account is that it
provides a natural accommodation of Dupré’s commitment to the
pluralistic way of delineating objects. Recall that Dupré’s realism is
pluralistic, meaning there are multiple valid ways of carving nature
into objects, and the way we choose depends on our explanatory purposes
and interests. The question I raised earlier was: How can Dupré maintain
a realist stance toward biological objects while acknowledging the
‘subjective, anthropocentric aspects’ and ‘pragmatic elements’ involved
in individuating them, without collapsing into instrumentalism?

Adopting Dennett’s (1991) framework offers a compelling answer to this
question. He emphasizes that real patterns are candidates for
recognition and are closely tied to the stance or perspective that the
observer adopts. This stance includes the observer’s cognitive
capacities, explanatory purposes, and interests. Dennett identifies
three primary stances we commonly use in both everyday and scientific
explanations: the physical stance, the design stance, and the
intentional stance. Adopting the physical stance might lead us to
identify patterns at the molecular level, while the design stance or
intentional stance might highlight patterns related to function or
goal-directed behavior. Importantly, the patterns themselves are
objective—they exist independently of the observer—but the specific
patterns we focus on depend on the stance we take.

This aligns well with Dupré’s pluralism. Different stances allow us to
identify different kinds of real patterns, each of which is valid within
its specific explanatory context. Consider Dupré’s example of a lichen,
a symbiotic organism composed of a fungus and photosynthetic bacteria.
Scientists delineate different individuals within this system depending
on their stances. An ecologist might treat the lichen as a whole,
focusing on its role within an ecosystem, while an evolutionary
biologist might focus on the population of bacteria or specific genetic
lineages as individuals undergoing selection. A microbiologist, on the
other hand, might zoom in further, treating each bacterial cell or
fungal hypha as an individual to study cellular interactions. Similarly,
a biochemist might define individuals based on metabolic networks,
viewing the lichen as a system of functional exchanges. Each perspective
identifies different real patterns within the lichen, and all are valid
within their respective explanatory contexts. Both perspectives are
legitimate and capture different aspects of reality, even though they
emphasize different patterns.

By grounding the delineation of objects in real patterns, we can
maintain a realist stance without denying the role of human perspectives
and pragmatic considerations. The objectivity of the patterns ensures
that realism is preserved, while the flexibility of stances accommodates
the pluralistic and context-dependent nature of scientific inquiry. This
approach avoids the pitfalls of instrumentalism by affirming that the
patterns we identify are genuine features of the world, even if our
choice of which patterns to focus on is shaped by our explanatory goals
and cognitive tools. A commitment to the real pattern account thus
resolves the tension: the choice of epistemic stance is pragmatic and
subjective, but the biological objects scientists delineate are always
constrained and grounded by the real patterns that objectively exist in
the world[15].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper has explored the tension between processualism
and scientific realism, particularly the challenge posed by the
object-oriented ontology that underpins many realist accounts in the
philosophy of science. Processualism, with its emphasis on dynamic
processes as the fundamental constituents of reality, offers a
compelling alternative to traditional substance ontology. However, its
compatibility with scientific realism has been called into question, as
realism typically commits to the existence of discrete objects with
intrinsic properties.

To address this tension, I have argued that effective realism,
particularly the real pattern account, provides a promising framework
for reconciling processualism with a realist stance toward biological
objects. By understanding objects as real patterns, we can maintain a
realist commitment to the entities posited by scientific theories
without abandoning the processualist ontology. This approach
acknowledges that objects are not fundamental but are instead
effectively real, grounded in their explanatory power and indispensable
role in scientific theorizing. The real pattern account, as developed by
Dennett (1991) and refined by Ladyman and Ross (2007) and others, offers
a robust way to understand how objects can be both real and emergent.

Patterns are objective features of the world, but their recognition
depends on the stance or perspective we adopt, which is shaped by our
explanatory purposes and cognitive tools. This pluralistic approach
aligns well with Dupré’s processualism, allowing for multiple, equally
valid ways of delineating objects depending on the context and goals of
inquiry. By interpreting objects as real patterns of processes,
processualism can maintain a realist stance without collapsing into
instrumentalism. This synthesis resolves the tension between
processualism and scientific realism, further defending processualism as
a compelling ontological framework for biology. Meanwhile, this paper
also showcases a further application of the ​real patterns account,
demonstrating how this Dennettian legacy can continue to profoundly
enhance our understanding of the fundamental reality revealed by the
sciences.

[7]  An anonymous reviewer has suggested that processualists could adopt
a related form of realism by treating ostensibly discrete biological
entities as “object-like processes.” On this view, the explanatory and
epistemic success of, say, cell-talk is secured by the claim that cells
are essentially processes that behave like objects (see also Dupré 2021a
for a similar proposal). Although prima facie attractive, I believe this
proposal demands further metaphysical elaboration in at least two
respects. First, if processes are fundamentally dynamic and relational,
what principled basis is there for carving out a single “object-like”
process from its environment? How can we justify drawing a boundary
around a cell given the ontological continuity characteristic of living
systems? Second, the position must provide a metaphysical account of why
processes need to approximate the hallmarks of objects in order to
underwrite successful reference or explanation. In particular, what is
it, within such a metaphysical framework, that renders “objecthood”
epistemically or explanatorily indispensable? In my view, only once
these questions are addressed can this framework be regarded as a
serious metaphysical option.
