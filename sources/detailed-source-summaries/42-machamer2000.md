# machamer2000

## Full Citation
Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, and Carl F. Craver, "Thinking about mechanisms," *Philosophy of Science* 67, no. 1 (2000): 1-25. https://doi.org/10.1086/392759.

## Detailed Summary
Machamer, Darden, and Craver argue that many scientific explanations, especially in molecular biology and neurobiology, are best understood as descriptions of mechanisms. Their best-known formulation is that mechanisms are entities and activities organized such that they are productive of regular changes from start or set-up conditions to finish or termination conditions. The paper's central intervention is to insist that an adequate account of mechanism must include both entities and activities. Accounts that analyze mechanisms only in terms of parts, interactions, or laws, they argue, leave out the productive character that makes mechanistic explanation explanatory rather than merely descriptive.

The article begins from scientific practice. In the relevant sciences, satisfactory explanation commonly means showing how a phenomenon is produced. This requires more than listing components or recording correlations. A mechanistic explanation traces productive continuity across stages. The familiar notation of successive stages, such as `$A \to B \to C$`, is not explanatory by itself; what matters are the activities represented by the arrows. If one cannot specify the activity linking stages, there is an explanatory gap.

Against "interactionist" accounts, especially those that analyze mechanisms as parts interacting according to direct causal laws, the authors argue that laws are often not what scientists actually use to characterize lower-level production in these fields. They also reject one-sided process ontologies and one-sided substantivalist ontologies. Entities matter because they are what engage in activities and because their properties constrain what they can do. Activities matter because mechanisms are productive. What explains is not just that a state changes, but what the relevant entities do to bring about that change.

This dual emphasis yields a broader philosophical picture. Functions are not best treated as static properties that entities simply have. Rather, functions are roles played by entities and activities within a mechanism. Causation is similarly activity-laden. It is not penicillin as a bare object that causes pneumonia to disappear, but what penicillin does. Laws, where they are available, describe regularities associated with activities, but they do not replace an account of productive organization.

The middle sections develop a set of methodological distinctions that became especially influential. Mechanisms are often nested hierarchically, with lower-level mechanisms serving as components in higher-level ones. Yet "bottoming out" is relative to a field, research program, or explanatory interest. Molecular biology and neurobiology typically bottom out at macromolecules, smaller molecules, ions, and a limited repertoire of activities, not at fundamental physics. The authors identify four broad kinds of bottom-out activity in these sciences: geometrico-mechanical, electro-chemical, energetic, and electro-magnetic. This relative-bottoming-out thesis is important because it rejects the assumption that explanation must descend all the way to fundamental laws in order to count as genuine.

The paper also distinguishes between mechanism schemata and mechanism sketches. A schema is an abstract, partially filled-out description of a type of mechanism that can be instantiated, used to explain, used to predict, and used to guide intervention and experiment. A sketch is more incomplete. It contains black boxes or missing productive steps, and so marks where further work is required. This distinction lets the authors describe scientific change as the gradual refinement, testing, revision, and sometimes abandonment of mechanistic hypotheses rather than as a simple contrast between finished theory and raw observation.

Their protein-synthesis case study illustrates that point. Competing biochemical and molecular-biological proposals each captured part of the mechanism but left crucial gaps or posited the wrong productive activities. Progress required identifying not only new entities such as messenger RNA and transfer RNA, but also the right activity, especially hydrogen bonding, to explain how amino-acid ordering occurs. The lesson is historiographic as well as philosophical: scientific change often proceeds through discovery of new entities and new kinds of activities, plus the replacement of sketches by better schemata.

The final sections draw out implications for explanation and reduction. Mechanistic explanations render phenomena intelligible by showing how they could be produced, how they are plausibly produced, or how they are actually produced. Intelligibility is historically and disciplinarily relative because what counts as an acceptable bottom-out activity changes across time and field. Reduction, meanwhile, is better understood in these sciences as piecemeal construction and revision of multi-level mechanism schemata than as deduction of higher-level laws from lower-level theories. Higher-level context remains essential to understanding the significance of lower-level activities.

## Core Argument Map
- **Starting problem:** Philosophy of science lacks an adequate account of what mechanisms are and how they explain.
- **Definition:** Mechanisms are **entities and activities organized** so as to produce regular changes from set-up to termination conditions.
- **Against law-centered views:** Mechanistic explanation cannot be reduced to direct causal laws or bare interactions.
- **Dualist ontology:** Both entities and activities are ontically indispensable; neither should be reduced away.
- **Productivity thesis:** Explanation requires productive continuity across stages, not just correlation or state-transition description.
- **Relative bottoming out:** Mechanistic inquiry stops at different levels depending on field and explanatory interest.
- **Schema/sketch distinction:** Science often works with incomplete mechanistic representations that are progressively refined.
- **Case-study payoff:** Discovery involves identifying the right entities and the right activities, not just more components.
- **Epistemic thesis:** Mechanistic explanation renders phenomena intelligible by showing how they are produced.
- **Reduction thesis:** Scientific change is better modeled as revision of multi-level mechanism schemata than as strict intertheoretic deduction.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper matters to RPNC mainly as a constraint on what a satisfactory higher-level realist account must respect. It highlights that many scientifically legitimate higher-level explanations are not just summaries of correlations. They depend on organized productive structure, multi-level context, and relative explanatory stopping points. That is useful for RPNC because closure should not be framed as mere predictive convenience detached from how systems are actually organized.

The relevance is nevertheless indirect. Machamer, Darden, and Craver are not offering a theory of real patterns, compression, or admissible coarse-graining. Their focus is mechanistic explanation in particular sciences. RPNC should therefore not assimilate mechanism to closure too quickly. The better use is selective: this paper shows why ontological and explanatory seriousness often requires more than extensional regularity, and why field-relative organization and productive continuity matter.

If RPNC invokes this paper, it should do so to reinforce that realist vindication of higher-level description cannot ignore organization, multi-level embedding, and causal production. It should not imply that every real pattern must be mechanistic in the specific MDC sense.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- The paper is explicitly centered on **molecular biology and neurobiology**. The authors suggest wider applicability, but they do not claim universal coverage of all sciences.
- They do **not** say that all explanations are mechanistic or that all sciences bottom out in the same way.
- "Bottoming out" is **relative to field and inquiry**, not a claim about ultimate metaphysical fundamentality.
- The account is strongly **anti-reductive in method** without denying that lower-level entities and activities matter.
- Use in RPNC should avoid treating this paper as a direct account of real patterns or as requiring that all admissible higher-level ontology be mechanistic.
