# simon1962

## Full Citation
Herbert A. Simon, "The architecture of complexity," *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106, no. 6 (1962): 467-482. https://doi.org/10.2307/985254.

## Detailed Summary
Simon's classic paper argues that many complex systems, across biological, physical, social, and symbolic domains, exhibit hierarchic organization and that this organizational feature is not incidental. A system is hierarchic in his broad sense when it is composed of interrelated subsystems, each of which can in turn be decomposed into further subsystems. The paper's central thesis is that hierarchy provides a non-trivial common architecture across many forms of organized complexity, and that this architecture helps explain both how complex systems evolve and how they can be successfully understood.

Simon begins by broadening the notion of hierarchy beyond formal authority structures. Biological systems, physical systems, social systems, and even symbolic artifacts such as books can all display parts-within-parts organization. The relevant boundaries need not always be spatial in the same way. In social systems, for example, the operative structure may be revealed by intensity of interaction rather than by mere proximity. This is already a significant step, because it suggests that complexity should be studied in terms of patterned organization among subsystems rather than as mere aggregation.

The famous watchmaker parable then illustrates why hierarchical organization matters for the evolution of complexity. Both watchmakers assemble equally complex watches, but one constructs stable intermediate subassemblies while the other does not. The watchmaker with stable subassemblies succeeds because interruptions destroy only a small amount of work. Simon's lesson is qualitative and general: the evolution of complex systems is dramatically facilitated when there are relatively stable intermediate forms. Complexity becomes tractable when it can be built in layers. He explicitly warns that the numerical toy estimates in the parable are only illustrative, but insists that the structural lesson is robust.

Simon extends this point to biological evolution and then to problem solving. In both settings, stable intermediate structures or cues of progress play the role of selective guides through otherwise intractable possibility spaces. This does not require teleology in any strong sense. Selection among partially successful intermediates is enough to explain why complex organization can arise without exhaustive blind search. The broader suggestion is that hierarchy and selective retention are deeply connected.

The paper's most philosophically influential section introduces nearly decomposable systems. In hierarchic systems, interactions within subsystems are often much stronger than interactions between subsystems. Where this is so, short-run behavior within a subsystem is approximately independent of other subsystems, while long-run behavior depends on them only in an aggregated way. Simon illustrates this with the thermal-equilibrium example of rooms and cubicles in a building. In the short run, temperatures equalize within rooms; only later does equilibration occur across rooms. This means that a compressed, subsystem-level description can be adequate for many explanatory and predictive purposes once the relevant fast variables have settled.

That idea then generalizes. Simon argues that nearly decomposable structure is common in social systems, economies, and physico-chemical systems. Input-output relations in economies, organizational communication patterns, and molecular bonding structures all display stronger local couplings and weaker inter-subsystem couplings. Because of this, subsystem-level descriptions are often not just convenient shortcuts but principled approximations licensed by the organization of the system itself.

The later sections turn from the evolution of complexity to its description. Complexity depends in part on representation. Many complex structures are highly redundant, and the right process description can yield a far simpler and more informative representation than a brute state description. Scientific understanding therefore often advances not by exhaustive listing but by finding the right higher-level representation of organized dynamics. Simon links this point to adaptive behavior and even to genetic encoding, suggesting that hierarchical and process-based descriptions are central both to science and to the systems science studies.

The paper ends by proposing hierarchy as one promising route toward a non-trivial general theory of complex systems. Hierarchies are common in nature, expected where complexity evolves from simplicity, dynamically simplified by near-decomposability, and descriptively simplified because their organization permits aggregation. The result is a foundational statement of why organized complexity should be studied through layered structure rather than through undifferentiated wholes or exhaustive micro-descriptions.

## Core Argument Map
- **Empirical starting point:** Many complex systems across domains exhibit **hierarchic** parts-within-parts organization.
- **Conceptual broadening:** Hierarchy need not be only formal authority; it can be defined more generally by organized subsystems and their relations.
- **Evolutionary argument:** Stable intermediate subassemblies make the emergence of complex systems far more tractable.
- **Non-teleological point:** Selection among stable intermediates can generate complexity without invoking strong teleology.
- **Problem-solving analogue:** Heuristic cues in search play the same role as stable intermediates in evolution.
- **Near-decomposability thesis:** In many hierarchic systems, within-subsystem interactions dominate between-subsystem interactions.
- **Dynamic consequence:** Short-run subsystem behavior is approximately independent; long-run dependence is only aggregate.
- **Representational consequence:** Aggregated subsystem-level descriptions can be principled and explanatorily adequate.
- **General conclusion:** A theory of hierarchy offers one route toward a substantive theory of organized complexity.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
Simon is an important background source for RPNC because he gives a classic argument for why higher-level organization can be objectively licensed by system architecture rather than merely by observer convenience. His discussion of hierarchy and near-decomposability is especially useful for motivating why some coarse-grained descriptions work: local interactions settle quickly enough, and cross-subsystem dependence is weak enough, that aggregate variables can track the behavior that matters.

That said, Simon does not provide a criterion of real patternhood in Dennett's sense, and he is not directly addressing the ontology question RPNC centers. His aim is a general theory of organized complexity, not a metaphysics of admissible patterns. RPNC should therefore use him with care. The strongest use is motivational and structural: Simon helps explain why layered organization and principled aggregation recur across domains, and why not every micro-detail must remain explanatorily active at higher levels.

RPNC should not claim that near-decomposability by itself is equivalent to closure. It is better treated as an antecedent structural insight: some systems possess objective interaction profiles that make compressed higher-level description possible and scientifically fruitful.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- Simon's watchmaker calculations are presented as **illustrative**. He explicitly cautions against taking the numerical details literally.
- The paper is **highly synthetic and exploratory**, not a worked-out formal theory of all complex systems.
- Near-decomposability is a **strong structural property**, though Simon argues it is common rather than rare in the natural and social world.
- The paper supports principled aggregation, but it does **not** by itself settle a metaphysical criterion for when an aggregate is ontologically real.
- Any use in RPNC should avoid identifying Simon's hierarchy thesis with a full closure account or with a general solution to anti-gerrymandering worries.
