## Detailed Summary
This document is a review of Hohwy's *The Predictive Mind*, not the book itself. The reviewer presents Hohwy's core picture sympathetically at first, then concentrates criticism on whether prediction-error minimization can explain how perception gets things right in the contentful sense of being true or false.

The review opens by reconstructing Hohwy's central claim. Perception is not passive reception of worldly input. Rather, the brain uses internal models to generate predictions and then updates those predictions in light of incoming sensory signals. The reviewer emphasizes what Hohwy presents as the major attractions of this picture: explanatory simplicity, broad reach across perception and action, and its grounding in prediction error minimization, Bayesian updating, and ultimately surprisal minimization.

The first substantive section explains Hohwy's framing of the "problem of perception." On this formulation, the challenge is not merely to explain how the brain processes input, but how it arrives at the right hypothesis about the world. The reviewer summarizes Hohwy's answer in standard predictive-processing terms. Hierarchically organized priors generate top-down predictions; bottom-up sensory input produces prediction-error signals; the system then iterates so as to reduce discrepancy between model and input. Precision-weighting is introduced as the mechanism by which the brain handles uncertainty and noise, and the review also explains Hohwy's distinction between perceptual inference and active inference.

The reviewer then notes Hohwy's attempt to use this framework to address longstanding issues in cognitive science, especially the binding problem. Hohwy's move is to reverse the standard problem: rather than binding raw unbound inputs, the brain predicts bound perceptual wholes and seeks confirmation from sensory input. The rubber-hand illusion is presented as an example of how sufficiently noisy or variable input can make an erroneous bound hypothesis minimize prediction error better than alternatives.

The review turns critical when it asks why this picture does not collapse into self-fulfilling prophecy. Hohwy's answer, as presented by the reviewer, is that prediction-error minimization is subordinate to surprisal minimization, which in turn keeps the organism within expected phenotypic states. The reviewer acknowledges this reply but says Hohwy leaves the relevant "expected states" underdescribed, so the deeper explanatory burden remains.

The strongest objection concerns content. The reviewer argues that Hohwy moves too quickly from covariance-based statistical structure to genuinely contentful states that can be correct or incorrect. A probability distribution may capture long-run covariation between brain states and worldly states, but covariation alone does not obviously yield representational content. Drawing on the covariance/content distinction associated with Hutto and Myin, the reviewer argues that Hohwy has not shown how statistical regularity can amount to truth-apt representation. At most, the predictive-processing framework as presented explains why a percept is statistically unusual, not why it is false.

This criticism is extended to Hohwy's claim that the brain "mirrors" the world. Mirrors covary with what they mirror, but covariance is not yet content. So even if predictive mechanisms track causal structure in a useful and fragile way, that does not by itself explain truth-tracking. The reviewer therefore argues that Hohwy has not yet solved the "problem of perception" as Hohwy himself states it, because the bridge from prediction-error minimization to getting things right remains under-argued.

The review ends by suggesting that the very framing of the problem may be partly responsible for the difficulty. If one starts with the picture of an isolated brain trapped behind sensory input, then one naturally seeks an internalist explanation of rightness. The reviewer suggests instead that the question may need to shift from "how does the brain get things right?" to "how do we get things right?", which would force a broader explanatory focus that extends beyond the skull. Even so, the review closes on a measured note. It grants that Hohwy succeeds in showing the remarkable scope of prediction-error minimization and its ability to organize many debates in cognitive science, even if the reviewer remains unconvinced by Hohwy's treatment of truth and content.

## Core Argument Map
- **Book under review:** Hohwy argues that perception, action, and related mental phenomena are organized by prediction-error minimization within a Bayesian hierarchy.
- **Problem formulation:** The key issue is how the brain selects the right hypothesis about the world.
- **Hohwy's solution as reconstructed:** Top-down predictions meet bottom-up error signals; precision-weighting, perceptual inference, and active inference reduce error and support perception.
- **Illustrative strength:** The framework is shown to bear on issues like binding, illusion, attention, and agency.
- **Main objection:** Statistical covariation and probability distributions do not by themselves explain representational content.
- **Misperception criticism:** Hohwy can explain unusual or low-probability percepts, but not yet why a percept is genuinely false.
- **Mirror criticism:** Tracking or mirroring causal structure is not yet truth-tracking.
- **Deeper challenge:** The link between surprisal minimization and contentful correctness remains unestablished.
- **Alternative suggestion:** The initial picture of an isolated brain may be misleading; a wider, possibly more externalist framing may be required.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This review is only indirectly relevant to RPNC, and it should be used carefully. Its main value is not as a statement of Hohwy's settled position, but as a compact critique of a familiar inferential move: from successful statistical tracking to robust representational or truth-bearing status. That concern parallels a reviewer-facing worry RPNC must address, namely, that predictive usefulness or covariance alone may be insufficient for ontological commitment.

The review is therefore useful chiefly as a cautionary analogue. It shows how an opponent can argue that formal success in prediction or compression does not yet establish the stronger philosophical claim one wants, whether that claim concerns truth, representation, or reality. RPNC can use this kind of pressure to motivate why closure needs to be stronger than mere predictive economy.

Still, the connection is limited. This is a review of a predictive-processing book, centered on content and perception, not on real patterns in Dennett's or Ross's sense. It should not be cited as if it directly advances or attacks RPNC's core thesis. Its relevance is methodological and dialectical rather than direct.
