# wallace2024

## Full Citation
David Wallace, "Real patterns in physics and beyond" (preprint, 2024), PhilSci-Archive. https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/id/eprint/23888/.

## Detailed Summary
This 2024 preprint revisits Dennett's real-patterns idea from the perspective of philosophy of physics. Wallace's starting point is the familiar problem of intertheoretic ontology inside physics itself. Different successful physical theories seem to describe the same world at different scales while quantifying over very different kinds of things: atoms, fluids, fields, phonons, electrons, and so on. He argues that neither disunity, nor straightforward mereological composition, nor instrumentalism gives an adequate account of these relations.

The initial appeal of real patterns, on Wallace's view, is that they capture something physicists actually do. Higher-level entities are not literally additional chunks of matter standing alongside lower-level ones, but nor are they mere fictions. They are dynamically and structurally real patterns in lower-level ontology. A fluid is not the mereological sum of atoms, but a pattern in atomic behavior made visible by the derivation of fluid dynamics from microphysics. Wallace therefore restates what he elsewhere called Dennett's Criterion: a macro-object is a pattern, and whether that pattern counts as a real thing depends on the usefulness, especially the explanatory power and predictive reliability, of theories that quantify over it.

He treats this as a major advance, but not a full solution. Real-pattern talk helps explain why physicists permissively introduce higher-level ontology without demanding strict composition. Yet Wallace argues that the criterion remains metaphysically unstable if left in its familiar form. If reality is tied to usefulness or to some physically possible perspective, then questions arise about the status of the criterion itself, the role of pragmatic considerations, and the possibility of interest-relativity. The real-patterns framework therefore needs supplementation.

That supplementation is provided by a version of ontic structural realism that Wallace calls "mathematics-first realism." His central claim is that physical theories are not best understood as natural-language inventories of objects and properties. They are mathematized representational structures, and natural-language ontology enters only when we produce what he calls a "predicate precisification": a way of describing, in object-property language, what a system is like according to the theory. Such precisifications are often underdetermined by the physics itself. A theory may license more than one equally acceptable ontology in natural language without any difference in mathematical content.

From this he draws a deflationary but realist conclusion about ontology. Ontology is "secondary" to mathematically presented structure. The fundamental success condition for a physical theory is representational and mathematical, not that it picks out one privileged natural-language decomposition of the world into objects and properties. Predicate precisifications are still valuable, because they help with understanding, pedagogy, coordination, and heuristic reasoning. But their claims are true, insofar as they are true, because they are good precisifications of a successful mathematized theory, not because they recover an independently fundamental object-language hidden behind the mathematics.

This reframes the relation between levels. When a lower-level theory mathematically instantiates an approximately autonomous higher-level dynamics, such as Navier-Stokes behavior within atomic theory, that is a non-pragmatic fact about theoretical structure. On top of that, we choose the most useful predicate precisification for talking about that higher-level dynamics, and this licenses talk of fluids and their parts. Wallace therefore treats ontology as scale-relative. At one scale we quantify over atoms, at another over fluids, and normally we do not need to quantify over both at once. The relation between these ontologies need not be captured by one universal metaphysical relation such as strict composition. In different cases, the mathematics fills in the relation differently. Real patterns is Wallace's proposed general label for the higher-level ontology that results.

The final section extends the discussion beyond physics in an explicitly tentative spirit. Wallace cautions that many non-physical sciences are not mathematized in the same way, and so the direct transfer of the physics model would be unwise. Still, he suggests that some apparently sharp ontological questions in biology and other fields may be artifacts of language. He then returns to Dennett's original target, the ontology of belief. Applying his framework beyond physics, he argues that beliefs and desires do exist where intentional-system theory gives a robust and reliable account of behavior. Their reality is not second-class merely because they are higher-level patterns. If one denies beliefs on those grounds, Wallace suggests, one would have to deny most of the ontology of ordinary and scientific life as well.

## Core Argument Map
- **Starting problem:** Physics uses multiple successful theories at different scales whose ontologies look radically different.
- **Negative diagnosis:** Neither disunity, straightforward composition, nor instrumentalism satisfactorily explains how these ontologies relate.
- **Real-patterns move:** Higher-level entities are patterns in lower-level structure and dynamics, not additional material items or useful fictions.
- **Dennett's Criterion:** A macro-object counts as real when theories that quantify over it are explanatorily powerful and predictively reliable.
- **Insufficiency claim:** Real patterns alone leave unresolved questions about the status of the criterion and the role of pragmatic considerations.
- **Structural-realist supplement:** Physical theories are fundamentally mathematized representations, not natural-language object inventories.
- **Predicate precisification:** Ontology enters when we render a successful theory in object-property language, but multiple precisifications may be equally acceptable.
- **Ontology as secondary:** Ontological claims are licensed by good precisifications of successful mathematical theories rather than by direct read-off from a uniquely privileged language.
- **Intertheoretic payoff:** When lower-level theory instantiates autonomous higher-level dynamics, higher-level ontology is legitimate as a real pattern in lower-level ontology.
- **Tentative extension:** Similar lessons may apply beyond physics, including to intentional states, but only with caution because the structure of those sciences differs.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This preprint is highly relevant to RPNC because it is a sophisticated attempt to say what real-pattern realism can and cannot do. Wallace is especially useful on two fronts. First, he insists that the easy alternatives are unsatisfactory: bare instrumentalism is too thin, while simple composition is too rigid and often mismatched to actual scientific practice. Second, he sharpens the thought that higher-level ontology should be tied to theoretically vindicated autonomous structure rather than to mere convenience.

At the same time, Wallace is not giving the sort of admissibility condition RPNC is trying to supply. His own discussion explicitly says that usefulness and predictive reliability do not by themselves settle the metaphysical issue, and his eventual answer turns toward a mathematics-first, structural-realist treatment on which ontology is a secondary precisification of successful theory. RPNC can therefore use Wallace as both ally and pressure point: he helps show why real-pattern discourse needs more discipline, but he does not himself offer a closure criterion that separates robust, dynamically self-supporting patterns from merely serviceable higher-level descriptions.

He is also helpful as a caution against overreach. Wallace's extension beyond physics is openly tentative, and his framework is shaped by the special features of physics, especially mathematization and clear intertheoretic structure. RPNC should therefore draw selectively from him: his treatment of autonomy, scale-relativity, and the insufficiency of bare usefulness is valuable, but his account should not be presented as already equivalent to a general closure-based realism.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- This is a **preprint**, not a final published article, and Wallace explicitly notes that it is a preprint version of a forthcoming chapter.
- Wallace's positive view is not "real patterns alone." His final position combines real-pattern talk with a mathematics-first structural realism on which ontology is secondary.
- The paper is focused primarily on **intertheoretic ontology in physics**. Its closing application beyond physics is exploratory and deliberately cautious.
- Wallace does not defend one universal metaphysical relation, such as strict composition, between lower- and higher-level entities. He argues that the mathematics differs case by case.
- His notion of **predicate precisification** is central. Any use in RPNC should avoid collapsing his view back into simple Quinean ontology-reading from theory.
- The paper does not provide a general anti-gerrymandering criterion for real patterns. It diagnoses the need for more discipline, but does not supply a closure condition.
