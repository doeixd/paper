# jiang2025

## Full Citation
Yihan Jiang, "Biological object as real patterns: Reconciling processualism and scientific realism," *Synthese* 206, no. 4 (2025): article 191. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-025-05285-z.

## Detailed Summary
Jiang's aim is to reconcile processualism in philosophy of biology with a scientifically realist attitude toward biological object talk. Processualism says that dynamic processes, not static objects, are biologically fundamental. But much of biological practice is object-centered, and standard scientific realism is usually read as committing us to mind-independent objects with at least some robust identity and causal significance. Jiang argues that this apparent conflict is real, but not unavoidable.

The paper begins by setting out the pressure from both sides. Processualists such as Dupré and Nicholson argue that biological systems are deeply interdependent, dynamically maintained, and often lacking clear boundaries. Jiang uses viruses as a vivid example. What we ordinarily call a virus includes different phases and transformations across a life cycle, and the boundary between virus and host can become unclear. This makes object-first metaphysics look ill-suited to biology. At the same time, biology constantly relies on object terms such as genes, cells, organisms, and viruses, and many philosophers explain the success of such science through realism about those objects.

Jiang then reviews the challenge this poses for processualism. Standard scientific realism, in the forms associated with figures such as Psillos and Chakravartty, tends to assume a metaphysics of discrete entities with intrinsic properties or causal powers. New mechanistic philosophy reinforces that picture by explaining biological phenomena in terms of organized entities and their interactions. If processualists reject object ontology altogether, they risk losing an obvious explanation for the success of object-centered biological theories.

The paper next turns to processualist attempts to respond, focusing especially on Dupré's cautious or qualified realism. Jiang's complaint is that these responses remain too underdeveloped. If biological objects are merely temporary stabilizations of process and if individuation is pluralistic and partly interest-relative, then a realist needs to say more about why this is not just instrumentalism. Without an account of what objective worldly features such object descriptions latch onto, cautious realism threatens to collapse into a merely pragmatic stance.

Jiang's proposal is to use effective realism, especially in the form grounded in real-patterns theory. He draws on recent work on effective realism in philosophy of science to argue that one can be realist about entities that are non-fundamental, domain-bound, and explanatorily indispensable. He then reconstructs the real-patterns tradition from Dennett through Haugeland, Ladyman, Ross, Wallace, and more recent authors. The key thought is that higher-level patterns can be objective and real because they compress information and are indispensable to successful explanation, prediction, and intervention, even if they are not fundamental objects with intrinsic essences.

On this basis, Jiang proposes a two-level picture. At the more fundamental level, biology is processual: reality consists of dynamic, unfolding processes. At a less fundamental but still realist level, biological objects are patterns of processes. They are not illusory and not merely convenient fictions. Rather, they are stable, objective, explanatory patterns that support counterfactual and nomological generalizations. Their reality is thinner and derivative rather than thick and substance-like.

Viruses provide the clearest illustration. Jiang argues that a processualist may be right that viral phenomena are fundamentally dynamic processes, but it is still often indispensable to describe those processes in object terms such as viruses, host cells, and their interactions. The reason this does not collapse into instrumentalism is that such descriptions track objective process-patterns that compress information and guide explanation and intervention. On this picture, viruses are real patterns of processes.

Jiang extends the point through a longer discussion of genes and developmental systems. Contemporary genetics no longer supports a simple one-gene-one-trait picture. Gene regulatory networks, environmental factors, and developmental interactions make genetic systems deeply context-sensitive and processual. Yet genetic practice remains strongly gene-centered because localized gene-patterns can function as reliable levers for explanation and intervention. Jiang argues that genes should therefore be treated as effectively real patterns within broader developmental processes rather than as ontologically basic objects.

The paper's final major payoff concerns pluralism. Jiang argues that real-pattern theory can explain how different ways of carving biological systems can all be legitimate without collapsing into anti-realism. Different investigative stances highlight different objective patterns. The stance dependence concerns which pattern is salient for a given explanatory purpose, not whether the pattern is merely projected by the observer. This is how Jiang tries to preserve Dupré-style pluralism while retaining genuine realism.

## Core Argument Map
- **Tension:** Processualism treats processes as fundamental, but biological science often succeeds through object-centered descriptions.
- **Problem for processualism:** If it rejects object ontology too strongly, it risks losing a convincing realist explanation of biological practice.
- **Critique of existing response:** Dupré-style cautious realism is too under-specified and risks collapsing into instrumentalism.
- **Effective-realist turn:** Non-fundamental ontology can still be real when it is domain-bound and explanatorily indispensable.
- **Real-pattern framework:** Higher-level objects are objective patterns that compress information and support successful explanation, prediction, and intervention.
- **Positive proposal:** Biological objects are not fundamental substances but real patterns of underlying processes.
- **Illustrations:** Viruses and genes are both better understood as process-patterns than as thick, self-subsistent objects.
- **Pluralism payoff:** Different scientific stances can legitimately track different objective patterns in the same system without making ontology merely interest-relative.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper is very relevant to RPNC because it is a recent attempt to mobilize real-patterns theory in exactly the neighborhood RPNC cares about: biological objects, process metaphysics, and the question of how non-fundamental entities can still be real. Jiang is especially useful where he argues that object talk can remain realist without commitment to thick substances and where he emphasizes explanatory indispensability, intervention, and objective patternhood.

For RPNC, however, the paper is best treated as a permissive real-patterns ally rather than as a full solution to the ontology problem. Jiang adopts real-pattern language largely in functional and effective-realist terms. That helps defend derivative reality, but it leaves open the familiar worry that usefulness and compression alone may not sharply distinguish admissible patterns from merely convenient ones. His treatment of pluralism also increases the need for a stronger discipline on which patterns count as ontologically serious.

That is where RPNC can engage the paper constructively. RPNC can agree that many biological objects are real as higher-level patterns of processes while arguing that closure is needed to explain why some patterns are genuinely world-anchored and non-gerrymandered. Jiang's paper therefore helps motivate the target, but it does not yet supply the stricter criterion.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- The source used here is a **preprint-derived text conversion**, and some wording may differ slightly from the final published version.
- Jiang relies on **effective realism** and **real-patterns theory** in a broadly functionalist way. He does not provide a strict closure-style admissibility test.
- The discussion of **viruses** and **genes** is illustrative of the proposed metaphysics, not an attempt to settle all empirical disputes in biology.
- The accommodation of **pluralism** depends on a distinction between objective patterns and stance-dependent salience. Any use of the paper should preserve both sides of that distinction.
- The paper is aimed at reconciling **processualism and realism**, not at defending structural realism in the stronger Ladyman-Ross sense.
