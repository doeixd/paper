# ross1995realpatterns

## Full Citation
Don Ross, "Real Patterns and the Ontological Foundations of Microeconomics," *Economics and Philosophy* 11, no. 1 (1995): 113-136. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0266267100003242.

## Detailed Summary
Ross argues that microeconomics should be understood as an empirical science, but not because it studies individual human beings directly under idealized rationality assumptions. His central claim is that the familiar ontology of individual rational agents is the wrong place to look for the empirical objects of microeconomics. The real empirical objects are abstract but worldly patterns, especially strategic patterns instantiated across concrete interactions.

The paper begins by reconstructing and criticizing Alexander Rosenberg's view that economics is better understood as a conceptual or mathematical discipline than as an empirical science. Ross grants that Rosenberg captures something real about economists' tolerance of weak predictive success, their interest in possibility proofs, and their distinctive disciplinary self-understanding. But he argues that Rosenberg moves too quickly from these facts to the conclusion that economics lacks empirical subject matter. The deeper issue, for Ross, is ontological rather than merely epistemological: what sorts of things could economics be about if not about individual rational agents taken at face value?

Ross rejects the naive view that microeconomics studies actual people as approximately idealized utility maximizers. He emphasizes that the rationality assumptions in standard theory do not behave like ordinary scientific idealizations. In mature empirical sciences, idealizations are typically revisable in light of data and are relaxed using independently justified principles. In microeconomics, by contrast, tastes and rationality conditions are often fixed in ways that prevent data from revising the core assumptions. This means that the rationality assumptions are not straightforward abstractions from empirical regularities. Yet Ross also rejects the conclusion that microeconomics is therefore merely conceptual or quasi-mathematical.

To make room for a different ontology, Ross draws heavily on Dennett's idea of real patterns. He presents Dennett's view as a way of resisting both strict atomistic reductionism and anti-realist or constructivist worries. A real pattern, on Ross's presentation, is a relatively stable and informative structure that can be detected from an appropriate perspective and that supports genuine explanatory and predictive gain. Patterns are perspective-dependent in the harmless sense that one needs the right theoretical standpoint to detect them, but they are not thereby constructed or unreal. The mind-independent structure of the world constrains what patterns physically possible investigators can find, and objective inquiry can determine whether a proposed pattern genuinely compresses and carries information.

Ross then applies this framework to economics. Economic theory, he argues, supplies a perspective from which real patterns become visible. This does not mean economic theory invents its objects. Rather, it makes visible abstract structures that were already there, much as sophisticated theoretical development allows one to identify inflation or equilibria where pre-theoretical observers would only see a confusing mass of events. Ross stresses that economic patterns are real even when they are only detectable under an economic description.

On this basis, Ross proposes that the proper objects of microeconomics are games. Starting from Robbins's definition of economics as the study of the relation between ends and scarce means with alternative uses, Ross argues that inquiry into this relation reveals recurring structures of strategic interaction, dilemmas, and coordination problems. These recurring structures are best understood as games in a broad sense. Game theory studies an especially important subclass of such patterns, but Ross's ontological claim is broader: microeconomics studies games as real patterns instantiated in the world.

Ross illustrates the point with the pattern known as "Matching Pennies," understood broadly as a type of game with multiple equilibria and Pareto-rankable outcomes. He treats various oligopoly and labor-market cases as theoretical examples of this pattern, then offers a more everyday illustration from Ottawa traffic behavior. The point of the traffic example is not that microeconomics is reducible to traffic theory, but that a recurring strategic pattern can explain and predict otherwise puzzling token events. The explanatory gain comes from recognizing the token interaction as an instance of a wider type. In that sense, the game pattern is doing genuine empirical work.

The paper ends by contrasting Ross's proposed research program with Rosenberg's. If microeconomics studies real patterns, then philosophers should ask which patterns microeconomists track, how models provide epistemic access to them, and how classifications of games should balance empirical adequacy with conceptual coherence. On this picture, microeconomics can make empirical progress by discovering new games, new classes of games, and new properties of games through a mixture of mathematical analysis and attention to real strategic situations.

## Core Argument Map
- **Starting problem:** Standard philosophy of economics cannot explain what the empirical objects of microeconomics are if individual rational agents are not the right objects.
- **Critique of the naive view:** Rational-agent assumptions are not ordinary idealizations that can be straightforwardly justified and revised by data.
- **Critique of Rosenberg:** From the weakness of direct prediction and the abstractness of the theory, it does not follow that economics is only conceptual or mathematical.
- **Ontological shift:** The key issue is not whether economics predicts quantitatively, but whether it has real empirical objects.
- **Dennettian resource:** Abstract patterns can be real if they are stable, informationally useful, and objectively detectable from an appropriate perspective.
- **Application to economics:** Economic theory reveals real patterns that would otherwise remain unnoticed.
- **Positive proposal:** The main objects of microeconomics are games, understood as recurring strategic patterns.
- **Illustrative payoff:** Recognizing a token interaction as an instance of a game pattern explains and predicts outcomes that would otherwise seem puzzling.
- **Conclusion:** Microeconomics is empirical because it studies real patterns, not because it directly describes psychologically realistic individuals.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This is a central background text for RPNC because it extends Dennett's real-patterns framework into an explicitly ontological defense of a special science. Ross is valuable not because he already provides a closure criterion, but because he makes vivid the exact kind of claim RPNC wants to defend: abstract macro- or meso-level structures can be empirically real without being reducible to the ontology of individual micro-particulars.

At the same time, Ross is also a useful foil. His account remains comparatively liberal. Stability, informativeness, and explanatory usefulness do important work, but they may still leave open the worry that too many strategically useful abstractions count as real. RPNC can present closure as a stricter admissibility condition that explains when a pattern is not merely useful from a perspective but dynamically self-supporting under the relevant transition structure.

Ross is therefore especially helpful in framing the move from "real pattern" to "real pattern with ontological discipline." He helps establish why economics and other special sciences should not be forced back into individual-level ontology, while leaving open the further question RPNC addresses: what blocks gerrymandered or merely instrumental patterns from entering ontology?

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- Ross's positive proposal is programmatic. He explicitly says he is sketching a research program rather than delivering a fully worked-out ontology of microeconomics.
- The paper relies on Dennett's real-patterns framework but does not itself provide a precise formal criterion for when a pattern counts as real.
- Ross is careful to confine the main claim to microeconomics. He does not claim to settle the foundations of macroeconomics here.
- The traffic and game-theoretic examples are conceptual illustrations of recurring structure, not stand-alone empirical demonstrations.
- Any use in RPNC should avoid implying that Ross already solves the anti-gerrymandering problem. He supplies the ontological opening, not the closure condition.
