# jiang2024

## Full Citation
Yihan Jiang, "The metaphysics of mechanisms: An ontic structural realist perspective," *Synthese* 204, no. 1 (2024): article 32. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-024-04684-y.

## Detailed Summary
Jiang argues that the dominant metaphysical accounts of mechanisms remain too object-centered. New mechanists typically define mechanisms in terms of entities, activities, and their organization, and the main metaphysical variants either treat entities as fundamental and activities as derivative, or treat entities and activities as equally basic. Jiang's complaint is that both options inherit a maximal metaphysics that assumes robust objects all the way down. That assumption, he argues, sits uneasily with contemporary philosophy of physics, especially ontic structural realism.

The paper begins by reconstructing the leading monist and dualist metaphysics of mechanisms. On the monist picture associated with Glennan, mechanisms are composed of entities whose causal powers or dispositions ground activities. On the dualist picture associated with Machamer, Illari, Williamson, and Krickel, both entities and activities are basic. Jiang emphasizes that both views ultimately need an account of where mechanistic decomposition bottoms out. If higher-level mechanisms reduce to lower-level mechanisms, then one eventually needs some fundamental level that explains why the regress stops.

This generates the paper's central problem: the bottoming-out problem. Jiang argues that object-based mechanistic metaphysics handles this by positing fundamental mechanisms made up of fundamental entities, or of entities plus activities, but this clashes with influential structuralist readings of modern physics. If the best metaphysics of fundamental physics does not support ontologically thick objects with intrinsic identity and properties, then a mechanistic metaphysics that assumes such objects at the base is under pressure.

Jiang strengthens the point in two ways. First, he argues that appeals to decoherence and an allegedly classical level do not solve the metaphysical issue. Decoherence may explain why classical description is often legitimate for practical purposes, but it does not establish a fundamental level of classical objects. Second, he argues that the bottoming-out problem is not merely philosophical because some areas of scientific practice, especially quantum biology, depend directly on quantum-level explanations. In such cases, a metaphysics of mechanisms cannot simply ignore the ontology suggested by physics.

The positive proposal is a structuralist metaphysics of mechanisms grounded in ontic structural realism. Jiang presents OSR as a minimal metaphysics committed fundamentally to modal structures rather than to objects with intrinsic identities. He then extends this picture to mechanisms. On his view, the metaphysical underpinnings of mechanisms are structures. Causal or modal force belongs primarily to structures, not to objects. Objects, if retained at all, are secondary and should be reconceived either as thin, context-dependent nodes within structures or as heuristic devices.

To make this proposal more concrete, Jiang compares it to dualist accounts of activities. He argues that what dualists call activities can be reinterpreted structurally. Mechanistic structures are best understood as inherently causal occurrents, that is, active, temporally extended structures. This lets him preserve much of what mechanists value about dynamic, productive explanation while rejecting thick object ontology. His suggested label, "entity-involving Occurrents," intentionally weakens the ontological status of the entity side.

Jiang then addresses the worry that biology and other special sciences indispensably use object talk. He does not claim to have fully solved the metaphysics of special-science objects, but he argues that OSR has promising resources. In particular, he appeals to Ladyman and Ross's real-patterns account as one way of accommodating objects such as viruses. He also suggests a possible rapprochement between OSR and processual ontology, since both are suspicious of ontologically basic, self-subsistent objects and both emphasize dynamic organization.

The final substantive section argues that structuralism is especially well suited to Mechanism 2.0 and systems biology. Here the mechanisms of interest are nonlinear, dynamic, and often mathematically modeled at scales where discrete object boundaries are unclear. Jiang discusses cases from neuroscience and active matter in which equations track changing quantities, flows, or system-wide behavior more naturally than an inventory of robust objects does. Structuralism, he argues, can read ontology directly from the causal-dynamical structures captured by these models, whereas an object-first metaphysics strains to fit the science.

The paper concludes by presenting OSR as a minimal metaphysics for mechanisms. Jiang's claim is not that all questions are settled, especially about how best to understand special-science objects, but that mechanisms can be made compatible with a physics-informed structural realism by taking structures, rather than thick objects, as fundamental.

## Core Argument Map
- **Starting point:** Existing metaphysical accounts of mechanisms are object-based and therefore metaphysically "maximal."
- **Problem:** Those accounts must explain where mechanistic decomposition bottoms out.
- **Bottoming-out challenge:** If modern physics undermines fundamental thick objects, object-based mechanism metaphysics lacks a satisfactory base level.
- **Further motivation:** Decoherence does not establish a fundamental classical ontology, and some sciences such as quantum biology make compatibility with physics practically important.
- **OSR resource:** Ontic structural realism treats modal structures as fundamental and reconceives objects as derivative or merely heuristic.
- **Mechanistic extension:** Mechanisms are underpinned by inherently causal structures, not by primitive objects bearing causal powers.
- **Accommodation move:** Special-science objects may still be retained in thinner form, for example as real patterns or context-dependent nodes.
- **Application:** Mechanism 2.0 and systems biology fit this picture especially well because their targets are dynamic, nonlinear, and often poorly captured by discrete object ontology.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper is relevant to RPNC mainly as structural-realist background rather than as a direct statement of a closure view. Jiang's main contribution is to pressure object-first metaphysics and to argue that higher-level scientific ontology should be understood through structures, modal relations, and dynamic organization. That is strongly congenial to an RPNC project that wants realism about higher-level patterns without reverting to substance metaphysics.

Its most useful contribution for RPNC is the way it links special-science metaphysics to constraints from physics while still allowing robust higher-level ontology. It also helps motivate why mere object lists are often the wrong ontological form for dynamic scientific domains. The discussion of Mechanism 2.0 and systems biology is especially helpful because it highlights cases where ontology tracks stable causal-dynamical organization rather than clear-cut individual objects.

Still, the relevance should be kept calibrated. Jiang is not offering a criterion of closure, nor a general anti-gerrymandering test for real patterns. The paper is primarily about replacing object-based mechanistic metaphysics with a structuralist one. Its appeal to real patterns is limited and programmatic. RPNC can use it as supporting evidence that structural and modal organization should do more ontological work, but not as if it already contains the specific closure condition RPNC wants.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- The paper is mainly a critique of **object-based metaphysics of mechanisms** and a proposal for a structuralist replacement. It is not a general theory of real patterns.
- Jiang remains deliberately **agnostic** between different ways of reconceiving objects within OSR. He rejects thick objecthood but does not settle the thinner alternative.
- The appeal to **real patterns** is brief and exploratory. It should not be cited as a fully developed special-science object theory.
- The discussion of **processual ontology** is suggestive rather than conclusive. Jiang explicitly treats reconciliation between OSR and processualism as future work.
- The applications to **Mechanism 2.0** and systems biology are meant to show fit and promise, not to establish that all such cases are ontologically settled.
