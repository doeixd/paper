# batterman2005infiniteidealizations

## Full Citation
Robert W. Batterman, "Critical phenomena and breaking drops: Infinite idealizations in physics," *Studies in History and Philosophy of Modern Physics* 36, no. 2 (2005): 225-244. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.shpsb.2004.05.004.

## Detailed Summary
Batterman defends the legitimacy, and in some cases the necessity, of infinite idealizations in physics. His main target is the view that the thermodynamic limit in statistical mechanics is merely a dispensable mathematical convenience, or worse, a source of philosophical confusion. Against Craig Callender's claim that appeal to the thermodynamic limit amounts to taking thermodynamics too seriously, Batterman argues that infinite idealizations are indispensable when the phenomenon to be represented involves a genuine physical discontinuity.

The first part of the paper revisits the relation between thermodynamics and statistical mechanics through the lens of singular limits. Batterman contrasts a philosopher's conception of reduction, where one theory is derived from another, with a physicist's conception, where one theory is recovered as a limit of another. What matters for him is that many such limiting relations are singular. When the limit is singular, behavior in the limit differs qualitatively from the limiting behavior of nearby finite cases. At critical points, this is exactly what happens in the thermodynamic limit. For finite $N$, the partition function remains analytic, so true thermodynamic nonanalyticities cannot be derived. This means that ordinary reductionist hopes fail at precisely the points where critical phenomena become philosophically interesting.

Batterman then reconstructs Callender's objection. Callender grants that the thermodynamic limit is pragmatically useful, but denies that it should be taken as foundationally indispensable, since real systems are finite. He suggests that statistical mechanics might instead rely on analytic partition functions for finite systems that somehow approximate the singularities used by thermodynamics. Batterman rejects this proposal. His core claim is that if the relevant limit is singular, then the idea of approximation at issue is not the ordinary kind. Smooth finite curves may look close to a singular curve, but that sort of visual or numerical closeness is not enough when the target phenomenon is a genuine discontinuity. If the world contains physical discontinuities, then a representation that rules out discontinuity in principle is not merely incomplete. It misses something real.

To support this stronger claim, Batterman distinguishes physical discontinuities from mathematical discontinuities. Physical discontinuities include the qualitative difference between phases at criticality and the topological break when a fluid neck pinches off into droplets. Mathematical discontinuities are the singularities in the equations used to represent those phenomena. His argument is not that every mathematical singularity corresponds to something physically real, but that in these cases the singular mathematical structure is tracking a genuine physical feature. Thermodynamics, on this view, is not mistaken to represent phase transitions as singularities. Rather, the singularity marks the failure of reductive smoothness across levels.

Mean Field Theory helps sharpen the point. Away from criticality, the thermodynamic-limit idealization can be relatively well-controlled. One can compensate for it and recover good approximate descriptions. But at criticality Mean Field Theory fails, precisely because fluctuations become dominant and the $N \to \infty$ limit becomes uncontrollable. Here the issue is not merely that the idealization is inaccurate. It is that the singular asymptotic regime introduces qualitatively new structure, which requires renormalization-group methods. These methods exploit scale invariance near the singularity and explain universality. The result is not a reduction of thermodynamics to statistical mechanics, but a vindication of the thermodynamic representation of critical behavior.

The second half of the paper extends the lesson by analogy to droplet breakup. As water drips from a faucet, the fluid undergoes a finite-time topological change. Near breakup, the neck radius becomes the only relevant macroscopic length scale, which motivates a similarity solution for the fluid profile. Batterman emphasizes that this hydrodynamic description is self-consistent above microscopic scales: the dynamics quickly forget microscopic details, and the same similarity structure appears across a broad class of cases. He notes caveats, including perturbative noise and regimes where universality breaks down, but the central claim remains. In both critical phenomena and breaking drops, singularities are tied to scale invariance and universal behavior. Infinite or continuum idealizations are therefore not optional conveniences. They are part of what makes a principled understanding of these discontinuities possible.

## Core Argument Map
- **Starting problem:** Many intertheoretic relations are understood through limits, but some of those limits are singular rather than regular.
- **Critical case:** The thermodynamic limit is singular at critical points, so ordinary reduction from statistical mechanics to thermodynamics fails there.
- **Target objection:** Callender argues that reliance on the thermodynamic limit takes thermodynamic singularities too literally.
- **Batterman's reply:** If real systems exhibit genuine physical discontinuities, then singular mathematical representation is not a mistake but a requirement.
- **Approximation point:** In a singular limit, ordinary approximation talk is insufficient because behavior at the limit is qualitatively different from nearby finite cases.
- **Mean Field diagnosis:** Mean Field Theory is useful away from criticality, but fails at criticality because fluctuations and scale invariance dominate.
- **Positive alternative:** Renormalization-group methods exploit the singular regime to explain universality.
- **Analogy case:** Drop breakup shows the same pattern. A continuum idealization and similarity analysis capture a real physical discontinuity.
- **General conclusion:** Infinite idealizations can be foundationally legitimate when they are needed to represent and understand genuine physical discontinuities.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper is relevant to *Real Patterns Need Closure* in a narrower and more cautionary way than the minimal-model paper. It strengthens the case that some macroscopic structures are not mere conveniences of description. They can correspond to robust, scale-stable, and physically significant phenomena that are not recoverable by straightforward bottom-up derivation from finite microdescriptions.

That matters for RPNC because it helps resist a reductionist reviewer move: the thought that any appeal to higher-level patterns is licensed only if finite microphysics can recover the same structure without loss. Batterman shows that, in some domains, singular asymptotics and universality are exactly what explain why such expectations fail.

Still, this paper should not be used as if it already supports closure. Its focus is on singular limits, infinite idealization, and the representational legitimacy of discontinuities. RPNC's stronger thesis concerns when a pattern is admissible as a real pattern because its transition structure closes appropriately. Batterman provides pressure against naive reductionism, but not the criterion RPNC itself needs.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- The paper is centrally about singular limits and infinite idealization, not about real patterns as such.
- Its defense of higher-level structure is tied to physical discontinuities and universality, not to a general anti-reductionist metaphysics.
- Batterman is not saying every mathematically singular model tracks a real singularity. His claim is case-sensitive.
- The distinction between controllable and uncontrollable idealization is important. He does not reject idealization wholesale, nor does he treat all idealizations alike.
- The droplet case is an analogy that supports the general lesson about discontinuity and scaling. It should not be collapsed into the critical-phenomena case.
- For RPNC, the safest use is to show why singular asymptotic structure can be explanatory and physically serious even when finite microdescriptions remain smooth.
