# franklinrobertson2024

## Full Citation
Alexander Franklin and Katie Robertson, "Emerging into the rainforest: Emergence and special science ontology," *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 14, no. 4 (2024): article 61. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-024-00622-4.

## Detailed Summary
Franklin and Robertson ask what should count as real within a scientific realist ontology of special-science entities. They reject two extremes. A sparse ontology tied only to the fundamental sits badly with ordinary realist commitment to entities such as viruses, gases, phonons, and mountains. But a permissive ontology that admits every non-fundamental fusion or gerrymandered kind also misses the distinction scientific realism wants to draw between scientifically indispensable kinds and arbitrary composites.

Their main target is the widespread thought that higher-level ontology depends on irreducibility. They argue that inter-theoretic reducibility is the wrong criterion for existence. Reduction is a relation between theories, not a direct test for what is real, and it is too unstable a basis for ontology. In particular, they insist that even if a kind can be understood from the bottom up, it need not be eliminated. Viruses are their central example: even if virology is in principle theoretically reducible to lower-level science, that does not undermine realist commitment to viruses.

In place of irreducibility, they offer an account of ontological emergence. An entity is emergent if and only if it is involved in dependencies that are novel and that screen off lower-level details. The screening-off condition is their core autonomy condition. Lower-level details remain unconditionally relevant, since the higher-level entity depends on them, but once one conditions on the relevant macrostate or macrodependency, further microdetail becomes conditionally irrelevant, either exactly or approximately. Their framework is explicitly Markovian in spirit. Macrodependencies can therefore underwrite autonomy without requiring failure of reduction.

The second condition is novelty. Franklin and Robertson do not require a strong, anti-reductionist notion of novelty. Instead, a macrodependency is novel when it is not type-identical to the microdependencies that instantiate it. In physics, this often appears as a distinct functional form. In biology, it appears as novel causal powers or at least a distinct causal profile. They connect this to cross-classification: higher-level kinds carve the world differently from lower-level kinds because the relevant dependencies cut across one another.

The paper's case study is tobacco mosaic virus. TMV is built from amino acids and proteins arranged in a particular way, and the authors grant that bottom-up understanding is plausible. Still, they argue that virus-level dependencies are emergent. Whether an active virus infects and mottles tobacco leaves can be predicted from virus-level properties while abstracting away from many details of the individual proteins. Virus-level dependencies also count as novel because the virus can do things its parts cannot do individually, and because virus-leaf dependencies are not type-identical to the lower-level dependencies among amino acids and proteins.

They then test the account against gerrymandered kinds, especially trout-turkeys. Such composites fail because there is no genuine higher-level macrodependency that screens off lower-level details. To predict what a trout-turkey does, one must keep returning to what the trout and turkey do separately. Arbitrary aggregates therefore lack the conditional irrelevance required for emergence. The authors acknowledge some vagueness at the edges, but they argue that this is not a special defect of their view and is common across scientific ontology.

In the final substantive section, Franklin and Robertson situate their proposal relative to Ladyman and Ross. They present their view as compatible with rainforest realism and with the idea that higher-level ontology is tied to real patterns, but they offer a more explicit metaphysical explication. Their account is less radical than some structural realist views because it does not require the ontological priority of patterns over entities. It also differs from Ladyman and Ross by placing more weight on novelty and screening off than on information-theoretic presentation.

## Core Argument Map
- **Problem:** Scientific realism needs a principled way to admit viruses, gases, and similar special-science entities while excluding arbitrary composites.
- **Negative thesis:** Inter-theoretic irreducibility is not the right admission criterion for higher-level ontology.
- **Positive proposal:** A non-fundamental entity is ontologically emergent when it participates in dependencies that are novel and screen off lower-level details.
- **Screening-off condition:** Lower-level details are relevant in general, but become conditionally irrelevant once the appropriate macrodependency is fixed.
- **Novelty condition:** The relevant macrodependencies are not type-identical to the microdependencies that realize them.
- **Case study:** Viruses satisfy both conditions because virus-level dependencies support prediction and explanation while abstracting from many lower-level details, and because they have a distinct causal profile.
- **Exclusion result:** Gerrymandered kinds such as trout-turkeys fail the screening-off condition and so are not emergent.
- **Conclusion:** Emergence, not irreducibility, supplies the criterion for admission to the scientific realist's "rainforest."

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper is directly relevant to RPNC because it is an explicit attempt to solve the permissiveness problem for special-science ontology. Franklin and Robertson want a criterion that admits scientifically serious higher-level entities while excluding gerrymandered kinds, and they do so by adding screening off and novelty to a broadly rainforest-realist picture.

For RPNC, the paper is especially useful as a close neighbour and partial competitor. It is stronger than a simple compression-based or usefulness-based appeal because it requires a form of autonomy and a non-trivial difference between macro- and microdependencies. At the same time, it does not formulate those requirements in closure terms, and its screening-off condition remains relatively permissive, especially where approximate screening off is allowed. RPNC can therefore engage it as a serious attempt to discipline real-pattern realism without suggesting that it already supplies the closure criterion RPNC defends.

The paper is also useful dialectically because it insists that reducibility does not settle ontology. That supports one of RPNC's central ambitions. Still, RPNC should not overstate convergence. Franklin and Robertson are offering an emergence condition for admission to special-science ontology, not a general closure-based account of real patternhood.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- The paper argues against using **theoretical irreducibility** as an admission criterion. It does not deny that some higher-level kinds may be reducible.
- The screening-off condition is explicitly allowed to be **approximate** in many cases. Any use of the paper should preserve that qualification.
- The virus case is presented as a detailed **conceptual illustration** anchored in actual science, not as a standalone empirical proof of the general metaphysical thesis.
- The authors do not claim that gerrymandered kinds are unreal in every sense. Their narrower claim is that such kinds are not part of the scientific realist's special-science ontology.
- Although the paper aligns with **Ladyman and Ross**, it is less radical than views that give full ontological priority to patterns or structures.
