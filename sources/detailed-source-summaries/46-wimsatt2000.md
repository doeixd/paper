# wimsatt2000

## Full Citation
William C. Wimsatt, "Emergence as non-aggregativity and the biases of reductionisms," *Foundations of Science* 5, no. 3 (2000): 269-297. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1011342202830.

## Detailed Summary
Wimsatt's central aim is to detach emergence from anti-reductionism. He argues that many philosophical accounts define emergence so strongly that anything emergent must be irreducible, mysterious, or hostile to mechanistic explanation. Against that picture, he proposes a weaker but scientifically useful conception: a system property is emergent when it is non-aggregative, that is, when it cannot be treated as a mere aggregate of the properties of the parts. On this view, emergence is widespread, compatible with reduction, and often precisely what reductive explanation reveals.

The paper develops this idea indirectly by asking when a system property is not emergent. Wimsatt identifies four jointly sufficient and separately necessary conditions for aggregativity relative to a given decomposition of the system into parts. These are: intersubstitution invariance (the property is preserved when equivalent parts are rearranged or substituted), size-scaling or qualitative-similarity invariance under adding or subtracting parts, decomposition-and-reaggregation invariance, and a no-cooperation/no-inhibition condition that rules out interactive effects among parts. If these conditions fail, the whole is not simply the sum of the parts, and some form of emergence is present.

This framework is explicitly decomposition-relative. A property may be aggregative for one decomposition and non-aggregative for another. That relativity is not a defect for Wimsatt. It is the point. The four conditions are meant to function as heuristic tools for discovering how systems are organized, which decompositions are more revealing, and where familiar reductionist simplifications hide important interactions. He therefore treats the analysis of emergence not as a search for metaphysical mystery but as a way of diagnosing organization, context-sensitivity, idealization, and approximation.

Wimsatt spends a great deal of time showing how easily apparent aggregativity is manufactured by idealization. His amplifier examples are central here. An idealized linear amplifier chain can seem to satisfy the relevant invariances, but only because one assumes exact linearity, serial arrangement, and stable modular boundaries. Once one changes the arrangement of components, examines internal decompositions, or relaxes idealizations to more realistic nonlinear behavior, the appearance of full aggregativity breaks down. The broader lesson is that unqualified aggregativity is rare and often a product of overlooked assumptions.

This has consequences for reductionist methodology. Because scientists and philosophers often search for decompositions that yield simpler and more context-independent regularities, they naturally privilege decompositions that exhibit partial aggregativity. Those decompositions then come to look "natural," and their parts come to look like the only real or important ones. Wimsatt argues that this slide from explanatory usefulness to exclusivity is a recurrent error. It produces what he elsewhere calls functional localization fallacies and fuels the familiar rhetoric of "nothing-but" reductionism: genes as the only units of selection, minds as nothing but neural activity, social behavior as nothing but individual behavior, and so on.

The paper therefore makes two connected claims. First, emergence, understood as failure of aggregativity, is extremely common. The conservation laws are unusual in picking out genuinely aggregative properties; most scientifically interesting properties depend on organization, interaction, and context. Second, this does not undermine reductionism in the mechanistic sense. A reductive explanation can show how a system property arises from organized interactions among parts without showing that the property is a simple sum of part-properties. Indeed, scientific progress often increases rather than decreases the recognized emergence of a phenomenon, because better theories expose more internal structure, more relational dependence, and more environmental sensitivity.

Wimsatt closes by connecting this to a broader account of scientific reasoning under partial ignorance. The four conditions of aggregativity are not just classificatory. They are adjustable heuristics for real investigators working with approximations, tolerances, incomplete decompositions, and shifting levels of analysis. Properly used, they help reveal the limits of models, the conditions under which simplifications are legitimate, and the systematic biases of vulgar reductionisms. Improperly used, they encourage disciplinary imperialism and inflated claims about what lower-level analysis has really shown.

## Core Argument Map
- **Target problem:** Philosophers often define emergence so strongly that it becomes automatically anti-reductionist.
- **Positive proposal:** Emergence should be understood as failure of aggregativity rather than as irreducibility.
- **Four-condition test:** Aggregativity requires intersubstitution invariance, size-scaling similarity, decomposition-reaggregation invariance, and absence of cooperative or inhibitory interaction effects.
- **Decomposition relativity:** Whether a property is aggregative depends on how the system is decomposed into parts.
- **Heuristic role:** These conditions help uncover organization, context-sensitivity, and more revealing decompositions.
- **Idealization warning:** Apparent aggregativity is often generated by strong assumptions and approximations that get forgotten.
- **Critique of vulgar reductionism:** More tractable decompositions are easily mistaken for the only real or important ones, producing "nothing-but" claims.
- **Compatibility thesis:** Mechanistic reduction can explain emergent properties without eliminating their organization-dependence.
- **Historical claim:** Better science often reveals more emergence, not less, because it exposes more interactions and contextual dependence.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
This paper is useful to RPNC because it offers a disciplined way to say why many higher-level properties are not exhausted by simple sums over lower-level parts. Wimsatt's analysis is especially helpful in resisting crude reductionist objections. A pattern may be mechanistically explicable and still depend essentially on organization, interaction, and context. That supports the general RPNC ambition to defend higher-level structure without appealing to mystery.

Wimsatt is also valuable methodologically. His four conditions function as a negative screen against easy but misleading decompositions. They make clear how gerrymandered simplicity can arise from idealization, forgotten background assumptions, or a privileged decomposition that suppresses interaction structure. That is highly relevant to any project, like RPNC, that wants to distinguish genuine higher-level patterns from artifacts of modeling convenience.

Still, the fit is limited. Wimsatt does not provide a closure criterion, and his account of emergence is intentionally broad. Failure of aggregativity shows organization-dependence, but it does not by itself show that a pattern has autonomous, self-maintaining, or dynamically closed higher-level structure. RPNC can therefore use Wimsatt as a valuable precursor and cautionary resource, but should not imply that non-aggregativity already delivers the ontological discipline that closure is meant to add.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- Wimsatt is defending a **broad and relatively weak** notion of emergence. He explicitly notes that, on his view, emergence may turn out to be the rule rather than the exception.
- The account is **compatible with reductionism** in a mechanistic sense. The paper is not a defense of anti-reductionist metaphysics.
- Aggregativity is always **relative to a decomposition** and often to assumptions, approximations, and tolerances. This relativity is central to the argument.
- Wimsatt insists that one cannot read aggregativity directly off the final equation. One must also inspect the derivation and the idealizations used to obtain it.
- The amplifier cases are **conceptual illustrations** of how idealization can create the appearance of aggregativity. They are not intended as standalone empirical proof of the general thesis.
- Any use in RPNC should avoid equating **non-aggregativity** with a fully general criterion of real patternhood or macro-level autonomy.
