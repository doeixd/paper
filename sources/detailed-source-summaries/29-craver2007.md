# craver2007mechanisms

## Full Citation
Carl F. Craver, *Explaining the Brain: Mechanisms and the Mosaic Unity of Neuroscience*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007.

## Detailed Summary
Craver's book develops a mechanistic account of explanation in neuroscience and uses that account to reinterpret issues about causation, levels, and scientific unity. The central thesis is that explanations in neuroscience are typically explanations of mechanisms. To explain a phenomenon is to identify the entities, activities, and modes of organization that together produce or constitute that phenomenon.

Craver's opening move is methodological. He argues that philosophical accounts of explanation should be constrained by actual scientific practice in neuroscience, especially by clear cases of explanatory success and failure. He presents the book as both descriptive and normative. Descriptively, it aims to articulate the explanatory standards that neuroscientists in fact use. Normatively, it aims to justify those standards and distinguish good explanations from bad ones. This emphasis on actual research practice is important throughout the book. Craver repeatedly treats explanatory norms as tied to what supports intervention, manipulation, and control, not merely to what produces a sense of understanding.

His core account of a mechanism is now well known: mechanisms are entities and activities organized such that they exhibit the explanandum phenomenon. The entities are the components or parts. The activities are the productive doings or interactions that the components engage in. Organization matters as much as entities and activities do; mechanisms are spatially, temporally, causally, and hierarchically organized. Craver uses the mechanism of neurotransmitter release as an illustrative case to show how neuroscientific explanation works by decomposing a phenomenon into organized component operations rather than by subsuming it under laws.

A major polemical target is the covering-law model and other non-causal accounts of explanation. Craver argues that these frameworks struggle with standard neuroscientific cases because they do not capture what makes an explanatory factor relevant. The key explanatory question is not whether a phenomenon follows from a lawlike generalization, but how it is produced or constituted. He therefore develops a causal-mechanical alternative, while distinguishing etiological causal explanation from constitutive or componential mechanistic explanation. The former concerns antecedent causes. The latter concerns the organized components whose activity makes up the phenomenon.

Manipulation plays a central normative role in this account. Drawing on Woodward, Craver argues that causal relevance is tied to ideal interventions: a variable is causally relevant to another when intervening on the first would change the second under the relevant conditions. He emphasizes invariance rather than universality. Explanatory generalizations in neuroscience are often fragile and highly context-sensitive, but they can still be explanatory if they are stable across the conditions relevant to the explanatory task. This is one reason the book is especially important for special-science explanation: it does not require exceptionless laws.

Craver extends the interventionist insight to interlevel explanation through his account of constitutive relevance as mutual manipulability. A component is relevant to the behavior of a mechanism when changing the component changes the whole and changing the whole changes the component, in the explanatory context at hand. This is not ordinary causal relevance, because the part-whole relation is synchronic and bidirectional in a way that standard cause-effect relations are not. The point of the account is to capture how neuroscientists test whether a lower-level item is genuinely part of the mechanism rather than a mere correlate or downstream effect.

The book also offers an influential treatment of levels. Craver argues that the levels that matter in neuroscience are levels of mechanisms, not a general metaphysical hierarchy of objects or sciences. Higher-level items are wholes or organized activities; lower-level items are their components. This framework allows him to resist simple reductionist pictures. Integrating levels is not primarily a matter of identifying kind terms across theories. It is a matter of showing how an item functions as a component in a higher-level mechanism and how that item is itself constituted by organized lower-level mechanisms.

The final large contribution is the "mosaic unity" model of neuroscience. Craver argues that neuroscience is unified not mainly by top-down reduction to a privileged basic level, but by the piecemeal integration of results from multiple fields that constrain possible mechanisms. Different fields contribute different kinds of constraints, including spatial, temporal, active, and interlevel constraints. This produces a patchwork or mosaic form of unity. What unifies neuroscience is the collaborative convergence of many investigative strategies on the same multilevel mechanisms.

Throughout, Craver's view is anti-fundamentalist in a qualified sense. He does not deny lower-level detail or physical constitution. Rather, he denies that explanatory adequacy is exhausted by moving downward. Good neuroscience often requires identifying the right phenomenon at the right level, locating its components, and integrating evidence across several levels at once. The result is a philosophically disciplined, empirically grounded account of how mechanistic special-science explanation works.

## Core Argument Map
- **Descriptive starting point:** Neuroscience already operates with fairly stable standards for successful and failed explanation.
- **Central thesis:** Neuroscientific explanations are typically mechanistic explanations.
- **Mechanism account:** A mechanism consists of entities and activities organized so as to produce or constitute a phenomenon.
- **Critique of non-causal models:** Covering-law and other non-causal models fail to capture explanatory relevance in standard neuroscientific cases.
- **Etiological relevance:** Causal relevance is tied to intervention and invariance under relevant conditions.
- **Constitutive relevance:** Interlevel relevance is not mere correlation or identity but mutual manipulability within a part-whole mechanism.
- **Levels claim:** The relevant levels are levels of mechanisms, not a single metaphysical scale of reality.
- **Unity claim:** Neuroscience achieves unity through piecemeal integration of multilevel mechanistic constraints rather than through simple reduction.
- **Conclusion:** Explanation, causation, levels, and scientific unity in neuroscience are best understood through a mechanistic framework.

## Relation to Real Patterns Need Closure
Craver is indirectly but importantly relevant to RPNC. He is not writing about Dennettian real patterns, compression, or closure as such. His importance lies elsewhere: he provides a rigorous account of when higher-level explanatory structure is legitimate without collapsing into either eliminativism or loose holism.

For RPNC, Craver is especially useful on three fronts. First, his distinction between mere correlation and genuine explanatory relevance helps clarify why predictive success alone is not enough for ontological commitment. Second, his emphasis on organized component structure offers a disciplined way to think about when higher-level descriptions are anchored in stable organization rather than in ad hoc summaries. Third, his treatment of interlevel relations shows how one can defend higher-level explanatory reality without requiring crude identity reduction.

That said, the fit is limited. Craver's framework is mechanistic and interventionist, whereas RPNC is concerned with closure conditions on pattern reality across a broader range of systems, including cases that may not present as canonical mechanisms in his sense. He is therefore best used as a source of methodological discipline and anti-reductionist structure, not as a direct precursor of the closure thesis.

## Reviewer-Relevant Cautions
- This is a book-length philosophy of neuroscience, not a general theory of all special-science ontology.
- Craver's target is explanatory adequacy in neuroscience; any extension to broader real-patterns debates requires an additional argumentative step.
- His account of constitutive relevance as mutual manipulability has been influential, but later literature has debated its exact formulation and scope.
- The book is anti-reductionist in one sense, but not anti-lower-level or anti-physicalist. It argues for multilevel mechanistic explanation, not for free-floating higher-level entities.
- Any use in RPNC should avoid suggesting that Craver already offers a closure criterion. He supplies norms for mechanistic explanation, not a general admissibility test for pattern ontology.
